Overview

Executive Summary

The core challenge of EchoYeast is to rapidly and reproducibly screen, from 17 nanobody sequences, the recognition element with the highest specificity for H1N1 HA and the lowest cross-reactivity risk, and to further validate the molecular mechanisms of two gating systems (hM1Dq and Ste2).

We established a computational-first / model-guided multiscale molecular docking and simulation pipeline:

  • Structure prediction using AlphaFold2/ColabFold;
  • Initial screening by multi-antigen rigid docking with ClusPro;
  • Semi-flexible refinement with HADDOCK 2.5;
  • Interface analysis with PyMOL + SPICE;
  • 100 ns MD with GROMACS;
  • Binding free energy calculation and per-residue decomposition with gmx_MMPBSA;
  • Positive control redocking with 9VH4;
  • Parallel modeling of the Path A (hM1Dq) and Path B (Ste2) gating systems.

By combining multiple orthogonal computational methods, we reduced the 17-candidate nanobody pool to a single lead (R1aB6) and identified a testable mutational hypothesis (R31A). Crucially, our model-guided design of the Ste2 gating construct was successfully translated into a living yeast chassis and verified via colony PCR and sequencing, completing the dry-to-wet lab design loop. This provides a prioritized experimental roadmap rather than a replacement for wet-lab validation.

1. Motivation and Design Need

EchoYeast requires a nanobody that specifically recognizes the hemagglutinin (HA) of influenza A (H1N1) virus with minimal cross-reactivity. We have 17 candidate nanobody sequences, but none of them have experimental structures in the PDB; moreover, screening them one by one by wet-lab experiments is both time-consuming and expensive.

Therefore, the model must answer the following scientific questions:

  • Among the 17 candidate nanobodies, which one has the optimal binding ability and specificity for H1N1 HA?
  • What is the binding mode between this nanobody and H1N1 HA? What are the key hotspot residues?
  • In the two gating systems hM1Dq and Ste2, how do the auto-inhibitory peptides bind the receptor? How does Gpa1 couple?
  • Which candidates should be prioritized for wet-lab experiments? Which should be eliminated?

We adopt a computational-first / model-guided strategy, using multiscale computational models to guide experimental design rather than replace experiments.

2. System Overview and Modeling Logic

2.1 Three main lines

Part 1 (Common Foundation): HA × nanobody docking — screening R1aB6
├── Path A (hM1Dq gating system): hM1Dq + MT1 + humanized Gpa1
└── Path B (Ste2 gating system): Ste2 + auto-inhibitory peptides + Gpa1

2.2 Logic Chain

Question (Which nanobody is optimal?) → Hypothesis (rigid + semi-flexible + MD can converge candidates) → Methods (ClusPro → HADDOCK → PyMOL/SPICE → 100 ns MD → MM-GBSA) → Outputs (specificity index / HADDOCK score / interface residues / RMSD / ΔGbind / hotspot residues) → Validation (positive control redocking + multi-method cross-validation) → Design decision (select R1aB6, propose an R31A mutant).

2.3 Seven-Stage Method Workflow (Part 1)

The questions, methods, and outputs of the seven stages:

Stage Question Addressed Method Output
1 Which antibody binds H1N1 best? ClusPro rigid docking (untreated antigen) Specificity index ranking
2 Does antigen processing affect the binding mode? ClusPro rigid docking (complex-processed antigen) Cross-validation
3 What is the cross-reactivity risk? ClusPro docking against SARS-CoV-2/HIV-1/RSV F Average binding energy, risk level
4 Can the binding mode be refined? HADDOCK 2.5 semi-flexible refinement HADDOCK score, cluster, BSA
5 Which residues drive the interface? PyMOL + SPICE Contacts, hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, hydrophobic interactions

2.4 Rationale for Method Selection

Our pipeline adopts a physics-based strategy, prioritizing interpretability and reproducibility. ClusPro[1] was chosen for fast rigid-body screening[2] across 17 candidates. HADDOCK was selected for its semi-flexible[3] refinement, allowing CDR loop flexibility based on our AlphaFold2 pLDDT scores. Finally, GROMACS[5] with the Amber99SB-ILDN force field was used to capture the explicit-solvent dynamics, which is essential for accurate MM-GBSA calculations. This multi-scale approach ensures that our computational predictions provide an actionable roadmap for wet-lab experiments.

2.5 Gating System Pathways (Path A / B)

Each pathway follows a unified workflow: Structure preparation → OPM/PPM membrane alignment[11] → HADDOCK docking → SPICE analysis → CHARMM-GUI membrane system → GROMACS MD → gmx_MMPBSA.

The radius of gyration (Rg) stabilized at 3.72 nm (90-100 ns mean), indicating a compact and stable complex without significant expansion or collapse during the 100 ns simulation.

Molecular Dynamics Engine: The Leapfrog Integrator in GROMACS. To simulate the atomic trajectories of our complexes over 100 ns, GROMACS numerically integrates Newton’s equations of motion. We utilized the Leapfrog integrator, a standard and highly efficient algorithm that updates velocities and positions in an interleaved, half-step manner:

Velocity update: v(t + Δt/2) = v(t − Δt/2) + (Δt/m) · F(t)
Position update: r(t + Δt) = r(t) + Δt · v(t + Δt/2)

Δt is the integration time step (typically 2 fs).

Interleaved Update: velocities are updated for half-steps, while positions are advanced by full steps, allowing the algorithm to “leapfrog” over the current state.

Efficiency & Stability: this method requires storing only one set of positions and velocities at a time, drastically reducing memory usage. Crucially, as a time-reversible and symplectic integrator, it prevents numerical energy drift during long simulations. This provides the rigorous mathematical foundation for the stable RMSD and RMSF trajectories we observed in our 100 ns MD.

Note on system size and simulation status:

Both atom counts (631,498 and ~850,000) represent the fully solvated systems, which include the protein/peptide, lipids, ions (KCl), and explicit TIP3P water molecules. The pure protein atom count is only a few thousand.

Why is the single-chain system larger (~850,000)? Although the hM1Dq+Gpα complex has fewer protein residues than the hM1Dq+MT1 complex, its required X/Y box dimension was significantly larger (188.52 Å vs. 150 Å). B.8 Model-to-Wet Lab Validation: Ste2 Gating Construct Assembly

Bridging Dry and Wet Lab: From Prediction to Construction

Our computational screening evaluated 8 auto-inhibitory peptide variants. Among them, one auto-inhibitory peptide (FHLLQLKPGQPMY) achieved the best HADDOCK score (−99.2). However, for the initial wet-lab assembly, we intentionally selected pep1 (CRGPQLKPGQPMY) as the baseline gating peptide. This decision was driven by the fact that pep1 shares an identical C-terminal core (QLKPGQPMY) with the native α-factor pheromone (WHWLQLKPGQPMY). This core sequence is critical for recognition by the native Ste2 receptor, ensuring optimal structural compatibility and membrane trafficking in vivo. The N-terminal extension of pep5 (FHLL...) improved docking scores in silico, but we prioritized the native physiological compatibility for the first wet-lab iteration.

The optimized auto-inhibitory peptide remains our top computationally designed candidate, and a direct comparative functional assay between pep1 and the optimized auto-inhibitory peptide will be conducted in the next phase of the DBTL cycle.

To demonstrate the practical utility of our Path B model, we translated the in silico gating concept into a physical yeast chassis. Based on our docking insights, we selected the auto-inhibitory peptide and fused it with an anti-HA nanobody to the N-terminus of the native Ste2 receptor.

Our model provided the exact sequence blueprint for this complex fusion. As verified in the wet lab, the construct was successfully integrated into the yeast genome. Colony PCR and Sanger sequencing confirmed a 96.90% sequence identity with our computationally designed reference.

The Closed Loop: This successful assembly bridges our computational predictions and experimental engineering. The model successfully guided the design of a functional, engineered yeast receptor, which is now ready for downstream signaling assays.

Limitations and Failure Analysis

Limitation Description
All results are computational predictions Not validated by wet-lab experiments; the ARG-B:31 hotspot and R31A mutant await SPR/ELISA validation
Single-chain simplification of hM1Dq + Gα Does not include Gβγ and may overestimate the conformational stability of Gα
Membrane lipid approximation Approximation of the BY4741 plasma membrane composition; real yeast membranes exhibit microdomain heterogeneity
Scoring function limitations ClusPro/HADDOCK scoring functions are not absolute binding free energies and cannot be directly compared across different targets
Implicit solvent/membrane environment ignored Docking was performed in vacuum/implicit solvent, without considering the membrane, water, ions, or glycosylation
Insufficient sampling 100 ns may be insufficient to capture slow conformational changes; MM-GBSA SEM 1.27-3.46 kcal/mol

Detailed Analysis of Failure Causes

  • hM1Dq + Gpa1 total CHARMM-GUI failure: The Gpa1 trimer was misidentified as a continuous long chain; a 25 Å long bond appeared between residues 512–513; minimization Fmax = 16839, ultimately Exit 139; fix: truncate residues 461–512 and switch to a single chain.
  • Accidental incorporation of CHL1 cholesterol: In one CHARMM-GUI run, ergosterol was mistakenly replaced with cholesterol (360 CHL1); corrected after reconstruction.
  • Membrane system size limitation: While the hM1Dq + Gpa1 trimer contains 584,325 atoms, the simplified hM1Dq + Gpα single-chain system expanded to ~850,000 atoms due to the requirement for a larger explicit solvent box. Due to computational resource limits and the estimated 2–3 weeks required for a 100 ns simulation, the full single-chain membrane MD was listed as Future Work.
  • Ste2 + Gpa1 coupling was not run: listed as Future Work.
  • The Ste2 + pep5 complex was simulated without its downstream Gpa1 coupling partner, which may affect the conformational sampling of the intracellular loops.

Membrane MD validation of G protein coupling

This work has completed membrane MD validation for two gating interfaces, hM1Dq–MT1 and Ste2–pep5. For the G protein coupling interfaces (hM1Dq–Gpa1 and Ste2–Gpa1), we have completed HADDOCK semi-flexible refinement and SPICE interface analysis to identify key coupling residues. Because the complete hM1Dq + Gpα single-chain membrane system required a larger simulation box, its total atom count expanded to approximately 850,000 atoms (including explicit solvent). Running a 100 ns MD for this system under our available computational resources would take 2–3 weeks, so we have listed it as next-stage work. The current HADDOCK data (Gpα single-chain score = −149.4, superior to total = −131.7) already support the conclusion that the coupling interface is primarily mediated by Gα.

Conclusion

The modeling work of EchoYeast embodies the core value of the iGEM computational-first / model-guided strategy. Starting from 17 candidate nanobodies, through a five-stage multiscale workflow of ClusPro → HADDOCK → PyMOL/SPICE → GROMACS → gmx_MMPBSA, combined with positive control 9VH4 redocking validation, we successfully converged on the optimal candidate R1aB6, identified the key hotspot ARG-B:31, and proposed a testable hypothesis, the R31A mutant.

In parallel, we advanced the modeling of two gating systems:

Path A: hM1Dq + MT1 (ΔTOTAL = −40.37 ± 1.27 kcal/mol, 50 interface residues)

Path B: Ste2 + pep5 (ΔTOTAL = −31.43 ± 3.46 kcal/mol, 63 interface residues)

Both gating interfaces (hM1Dq–MT1 and Ste2–pep5) have completed 100 ns membrane MD, MM-GBSA, and per-residue decomposition, and through SPICE interface analysis revealed the specific interaction networks, providing atomic-level structural support for the whole-cell biosensor of EchoYeast.

The value of the model lies not in replacing wet-lab experiments, but in providing precise targets and prioritization for them — this is precisely the direction advocated by the iGEM Best Model Award. All simulation inputs, parameter files, and analysis scripts are available at the GitHub repository, ensuring full reproducibility of the computational workflow.
