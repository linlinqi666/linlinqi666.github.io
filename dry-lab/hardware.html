Hardware

Overview

The Echo Yeast temperature-controlled incubation and optical readout module.

This page documents the device we designed and built for the PAGER-Yeast biosensor: a temperature-controlled incubation and optical readout module that keeps the engineered yeast at 30 °C, excites the green fluorescent protein they express at 485 nm, and reads the 528 nm emission back as an electrical signal.

The sampling cup is also the culture cup and the detection cell.

Influenza arrives every year, and the way we watch for it is arranged almost entirely around people: someone develops a fever or a cough, a swab is taken, the sample goes to a laboratory, and a result comes back later. There is nothing wrong with that chain — it simply starts a little too late. By the time the result exists, the virus may already have made its way through classrooms, wards and waiting halls.

This module is our answer to the half of that chain that never reaches a laboratory in time. It is designed to sit next to a wet-cyclone sampler, hold the yeast that does the sensing, and turn their fluorescence into a reading that does not have to travel.

Problem Statement

Waiting for someone to run a fever

Influenza A virus (IAV) is a persistent global public-health threat. WHO estimates that seasonal influenza causes around 1 billion infections, 3–5 million cases of severe illness and 290,000–650,000 respiratory deaths each year, with children, older adults and immunocompromised people carrying the heaviest burden [1]. The surface haemagglutinin (HA) protein continually escapes existing population immunity through antigenic drift and shift, so vaccine strains always lag behind circulating strains: the virus keeps changing, while our window on it opens only once symptoms appear.

Starting from symptoms decides what such a chain can and cannot record. A throat swab can only be taken from someone already feverish or coughing; before that point, the particles they exhale are already in the air of the same room. This is not speculation: air-sampling studies have recovered influenza A virus RNA from indoor air in a health centre and a day-care centre, at total concentrations of 5.8×10³–3.7×10⁴ genome copies per cubic metre, with about 64% of the viral load carried on fine particles below 2.5 µm that can stay suspended for hours [2]. Patient-centred surveillance therefore carries a built-in delay — it records what has happened, and does not announce what is about to.

The half that travels through the air

Bringing air into the picture is what closes that gap. The most direct gain is pre-symptomatic warning: detecting virus in indoor air before large numbers of clinical cases appear gives control measures a head start — ventilation, disinfection and crowd management can be scheduled before infections happen rather than after.

It also puts risk in its proper place. Within one building, classrooms, wards and waiting halls do not carry the same risk, and air monitoring can tell which zones are the hot spots, so that limited effort goes where it is needed. For national influenza networks, built around clinical patients, this adds the environmental dimension that trend assessment currently lacks. And in the settings that carry the heaviest burden — schools and care homes — a real-time air risk assessment bears directly on reducing cluster outbreaks.

Diluted almost to the point of disappearing

Finding virus in air is not primarily a question of whether it can be recognised, but of whether enough sample can be obtained. Viral particles occur at very low concentration in ambient air, and a sampler can process only a limited volume of it; multiply the two and what reaches the liquid is a very small amount indeed.

The accepted paradigm is a two-step workflow: a bioaerosol sampler collects airborne particles on site, and the sample is then sent back to a central laboratory for qRT-PCR. It is sensitive and dependable, but it splits sampling from detection across two places and two moments, with transport and laboratory turnaround in between, so what comes back is usually retrospective data rather than a field result [3]. Sampling itself also loses material: the collection efficiency of cyclone samplers varies widely with design and falls at high flow rates as the collection liquid evaporates and already-captured particles are re-aerosolised [4]. No mature low-cost option exists for on-site airborne IAV detection — that gap is what we set out to fill.

Choosing what emits the signal

The next question is what should emit the signal. The familiar answer is a chemical reagent: a chromogenic substrate, an antibody, an enzyme label. These are mature and reproducible, but under conditions that here hold at the same time — airborne IAV, on site, low cost, continuous — each of them is constrained in its own way.

Set against those four constraints, each method is either not sensitive enough, or tied to a laboratory, or dependent on a cold chain and batch-to-batch comparison. That led us to change how the signal is generated: instead of an added colour reaction or an antibody, we let a living cell report what it has sensed.

Handing the amplification to the cell

The most substantive advantage of using yeast is that recognition and amplification can happen inside one cell. The recognition element is a genetically encoded protein: the anti-HA nanobody is carried on a plasmid and copied along with the cell, so every yeast cell produces its own reagent and no antibody cold chain or batch comparison is needed [5].

The cell also amplifies the signal. One receptor activated by one antigen is turned, through the GPCR–MAPK cascade, into transcription of a large amount of fluorescent protein from the FUS1 promoter — a classic strongly pheromone-inducible promoter [11] — a level of biological amplification a colour reaction cannot provide. Yeast is a natural match for this route as well: it already possesses a G-protein-coupled receptor → Gα → MAPK pathway, which is precisely the interface an engineered GPCR needs, something a bacterial chassis cannot offer [6].

The output end is simpler for the same reason: yEGFP is a fluorescent protein the yeast synthesises itself, so no chromogenic substrate has to be added to the sampling liquid and there is no substrate to be diluted or degraded [12]. The chassis is also a reassuring one — BY4741 is a non-pathogenic biosafety-level-1 yeast, and its auxotrophic markers add a further containment barrier, since the engineered strain cannot proliferate stably in a natural environment. There is a further consequence: retargeting means replacing only the nanobody domain, while the heating and optical hardware stays exactly as it is.

The few changes written into the genome

Wild-type yeast, however, neither recognises the HA of human influenza virus nor accepts an engineered GPCR. To turn antigen binding into gene expression, three changes at the genetic level are needed.

The first releases the pathway's own brake: knocking out FAR1 and SST2 removes the G1 cell-cycle arrest and the negative regulation imposed by the mating pheromone pathway, so that the signal can be sustained and stable [6].

The second gives the yeast a new interface: building a Gpa1–Gαq chimeric G protein by replacing the five C-terminal residues of yeast Gpa1 (KIGII) with the human Gαq sequence EYNLV, so that the yeast G protein can be recognised and activated by a human-derived receptor and the heterologous receptor is coupled into the yeast MAPK pathway [8].

The third installs a switch with very low background: assembling the PAGER fusion receptor (MT1 auto-inhibitory peptide – anti-HA nanobody – TEV site – ALFA tag – hM1Dq) translates "HA bound" into "inhibition relieved", so that the receptor stays closed when no antigen is present and opens only when there is [7].

None of this can be done with a chemical reagent. An antibody or a chromogenic substrate reports only the instantaneous event of binding or not binding; it cannot convert that event into ongoing gene expression inside a cell, nor be copied and amplified as the cell divides. We are not merely swapping one recognition molecule for another — we hand both recognition and amplification to the same living cell.

We should be explicit about one point: the only small molecule that must be added is the agonist DCZ, which goes into the medium with the yeast before sampling and supplies the activating ligand for the GPCR. Recognition itself is entirely genetically encoded and requires no antibody or enzyme-labelled reagent. The hM1Dq receptor used here comes from the DREADD family [9], and DCZ is a later, high-affinity agonist that acts at low doses [10].

What the Hardware Is Worth: Folding the Whole Chain into One Cup

The performance ceiling of a biosensor is set by its molecular design, but whether it can actually be used depends on whether the hardware can fit that chain into a carryable box. We do not treat the hardware as an incubator bolted onto a yeast sensor. Its job is to compress the steps that normally live in a sampler, a sample tube, a transport box and a laboratory instrument into a single cup.

Table 2. The conventional aerosol-sampling-plus-laboratory workflow against what this hardware folds into one cup.
StepConventional paradigm (aerosol sampling + laboratory qRT-PCR)Our hardware
Sampling A sampler collects airborne particles onto liquid or a filter The cyclone spins airborne particles straight into the yeast medium in the cup
Sample transfer Transfer to a tube, labelling, cold-chain transport Not needed: the sampling cup is the culture cup and the detection cell
Signal generation Nucleic acid extracted and amplified in a laboratory Engineered yeast in the cup are induced by the antigen and express the fluorescent protein themselves
Readout qPCR instrument in a laboratory 485 nm excitation / 528 nm collection at 90°, converted to an electrical signal on site
Time to result Hours to 1–2 days including transport and queueing Sampling and expression overlap; total time on the order of hours
Cost per test Reagents + instrument amortisation + specialist labour Medium + disposable structural parts (the yeast amplifies itself)
Retargeting Redesign primers and probes, revalidate Replace the nanobody domain only; heating and optics hardware unchanged
  • Sampling cup = culture cup = detection cell: the cyclone drives airborne particles straight into the yeast medium inside the cup, so the sample never has to be transferred to another vessel. Transfer loss, transfer contamination and sample mislabelling disappear at the source.
  • Readout on site: 485 nm excitation and 528 nm collection at 90°, converted to an electrical signal there and then. This is the most fundamental difference between a field-deployable method and a laboratory method.
  • Overlapping in time: the yeast is in the cup from the start, so while sampling proceeds it is simultaneously being induced and simultaneously synthesising fluorescent protein. Sampling time and expression time overlap instead of adding up.
  • Integrated over volume and time: the cyclone keeps enriching airborne virus into a limited liquid volume while the yeast keeps accumulating fluorescent protein — low instantaneous concentration is integrated, in both space and time, into a measurable fluorescence intensity.
  • A different cost structure: the cost of qRT-PCR lies in reagents, instrument amortisation and specialist labour; ours lies in disposable structural parts, an LED, a photodiode and culture medium — and the "reagent" grows itself.
  • Retargeting without touching the hardware: specificity is set by the nanobody, so changing target means changing strain while heating and optics are reused as they are.

One honest caveat: co-culture means the cup is aerated throughout sampling, which can bring foaming, evaporation and contamination, and the liquid surface must be allowed to settle before readout. These are exactly the points our test stage is designed to quantify, and they are listed in the test plan in Section 10.

How We Got Here: From Wet-Cyclone Collection to a Cup of Glowing Medium

This hardware began with a concrete observation. While reading up on how airborne bioaerosols are sampled, we came across the wet-cyclone principle: air is spun at high speed inside a chamber, aerosol particles are driven to the wall by centrifugal force, captured by a liquid film on that wall, and end up as liquid in a collection cup. The technique exists in order to collect what is in the air and hand it on for later analysis.

We paused there. If the endpoint of collection is a cup of liquid anyway, why should that liquid only ever be water or buffer? If we replace the collection liquid with medium containing our yeast, sampling and detection can happen in the same cup.

How the process runs

Following that thought, the whole process falls into eight steps:

  1. The device draws in air, and the cyclone spins airborne particles — including any influenza A virus present — into the liquid in the cup.
  2. That liquid is our engineered yeast culture, and the yeast cells carry the PAGER fusion receptor on their surface, whose nanobody domain recognises the HA protein on the influenza A virion.
  3. When HA binds the nanobody, steric hindrance displaces the auto-inhibitory peptide MT1 from the hM1Dq receptor, relieving the receptor's self-inhibition.
  4. In the presence of the agonist DCZ, which is already in the cup with the yeast before sampling starts, the de-repressed receptor activates the yeast MAPK pathway through the Gpa1–Gαq chimeric G protein, and the FUS1 promoter is switched on.
  5. FUS1 drives transcription of yEGFP, and the yeast begins to synthesise green fluorescent protein.
  6. We shine 485 nm excitation light into the sampling cup, exciting the fluorescent protein that has already been expressed.
  7. The fluorescent protein emits at 528 nm, collected by a receiver placed at 90° in space to the excitation path.
  8. The receiver converts the optical signal into an electrical signal, and from that signal we judge whether influenza A virus is present in this air sample.

The 90° angle is not arbitrary. The excitation beam is strong and the fluorescence is weak, and their centre wavelengths differ by only about 43 nm. If the receiver sat on the same axis as the emitter, scattered excitation light would simply swamp the fluorescence. Turning it 90° takes the main excitation beam out of the receiver's direction, and two narrow-band filters then separate that 43 nm properly.

One more point is worth stating. DCZ sits in the same liquid as the yeast from before sampling begins, so the switch of this system is in practice thrown by HA alone: without HA the receptor stays self-inhibited and the pathway is closed, and only when HA is present is that inhibition relieved. DCZ effectively presets the switch to a state in which it can be opened, and the discriminating signal comes from the antigen itself.

The three blocks in the hardware

The hardware therefore falls into three blocks, each matching one stretch of that flow: the heated block keeps the yeast at its optimum 30 °C; the detachable optical plugs provide the 485 nm excitation and 528 nm collection paths; and the signal-acquisition circuit turns the nanoampere-to-microampere photocurrent from the photodiode into a number we can act on.

Design Goals and Constraints

  • Temperature: yeast protein expression and signalling are temperature sensitive, and drift directly changes the amplitude of the fluorescence signal. Control accuracy is therefore specified as ±0.5 °C with a set point of 30 °C.
  • Optics: yEGFP is a codon-optimised green fluorescent protein reporter for yeast, conventionally excited with blue light (about 485 nm) and read out in the green channel (about 528 nm) [12], only ~43 nm apart. Narrow-band filters (30 nm FWHM, OD3) are required to separate excitation from emission, and the excitation and collection axes are placed at 90° to suppress direct excitation breakthrough geometrically.
  • Sample volume: ≥ 1.5 mL at the cuvette stage; at the collection-cup stage the cup is pre-filled with 20 mL of medium containing the yeast, which brings the liquid level just above the junction between the cone and the cylinder.
  • Light sealing: we measure microampere-level photocurrent from a photodiode, and ambient light is the dominant noise source. Light sealing is therefore a structural requirement rather than an accessory.
  • Serviceability: optical components and the vessel must come apart, be replaceable and be cleanable — this is exactly why the emitter and receiver are plug-in parts.
  • Fabrication: with no precision machining available at first, FDM 3D printing is used for fit and assembly checks, and CNC machining is used for the formal test parts.

Design Iterations

The collection cup: from AutoCAD to SolidWorks

14 September — the first collection cup was drawn in AutoCAD: long overall (about 136 mm) with a skirt at the base acting as a support (Fig. 1), so the cup could stand on its own.

21 September — the first 3D model was built in SolidWorks: the supporting skirt was removed, the conical section shortened and the bottom cone radius increased for a smoother shape (Figs. 2–3).

22 September — the second version added optical windows on the conical face (Figs. 4–6). The reason is practical: the cyclone collects only a small volume, so the liquid level sits low. Windows on the cylindrical section would be above the liquid and the optical path would be empty; on the cone they stay submerged even with a small volume. In the current filling scheme the cup is pre-filled with 20 mL of medium containing the yeast, which brings the level just above the cone-to-cylinder junction; because the windows sit on the cone, they keep a margin of immersion even if the level falls through evaporation or sampling.

The heated block

25 September — the heated block was modelled with a cavity sized to the collection cup and optical channels reserved for detection (Figs. 7–8).

4 October — the optical channel was revised to meet real hardware needs: widened to 8 mm, with the filters, lenses, LED and photodiode all made detachable; EH2.0 was finalised and printed (Figs. 13–16). The original channel was too narrow for the components and left no room for filters; 8 mm accommodates Ø10 mm filters and lens assemblies while still leaving alignment margin.

Detachable plugs: emitter, receiver and heater

The emitter and receiver ends were drawn on 4 October (Figs. 17–18), and Emitter_Plug, Receiver_Plug and Heater_Plug were completed on 9 October (Figs. 21–26).

These three plugs are not ad-hoc parts for a single test but purpose-designed structures for the project: the emitter holds the 485 nm LED, the focusing lens and the excitation filter; the receiver holds the emission filter, the focusing lens and the BPW34 photodiode; the heater plug routes the heating-film leads and the PT100 into the block while keeping light out and assembly simple.

For the test stage we replaced the in-house collection cup with a standard cuvette and redesigned the heated block around it. A cuvette is a standard part with consistent dimensions and flat optical faces, which lets us validate temperature control and the optical path before the custom parts are finalised. The plug-to-block interface is identical across revisions, so switching back to our own collection cup requires no redesign of the plugs.

3D printing and assembly checks

The first cup was printed on 30 September and came out poorly because of print settings and our inexperience (Figs. 9–10). On 2 October the hardware module was printed for the first time — supports still inside the optical channel — and the cup was assembled with the heated block to confirm the dimensions (Figs. 11–12). This was the first time our 3D models became physical parts, and the assembly fitted. EH2.0 was printed on 4 October and re-checked with the cup (Figs. 15–16).

Electronics

The first circuit was designed on 4 October (Fig. 19); soldering began on 5 October with the aim of finishing the wiring around the 10th and running the first test (Fig. 20); the wiring was rearranged on 11 October to keep every component within its safe operating range (Fig. 27).

The circuit is organised by function: power (24 V input stepped down by an LM2596), temperature control (PT100 + MAX31865 + IRF520 + heating film), LED drive (constant-current board + 485 nm LED), signal (BPW34 + OPA350 transimpedance amplifier + ADS1115) and indication.

Optical and Electronic Architecture

Optical path

485 nm LED → focusing lens → 485 nm narrow-band filter → vessel (cuvette / collection cup) → 528 nm narrow-band filter → focusing lens → BPW34 photodiode → OPA350 transimpedance amplifier → ADS1115 (16-bit, I²C).

Temperature control

PT100 (3-wire) → MAX31865 (16-bit, SPI) → PID loop on the microcontroller → IRF520 (PWM) → PI heating film, with a KSD9700 (60 °C) in series with the heater as hardware-level protection.

Power

24 V adapter → LM2596 → 12 V (heating film, fan) and 5 V (logic). Power and signal wiring are routed separately so that switching noise does not couple into the microampere-level photocurrent.

The Complete Instrument and the Overall Plan

Beyond the modules, we also have a full set of technical requirements for the instrument as a whole: a wet-wall cyclone bioaerosol sampling and optical detection instrument that integrates cyclone sampling, a COC collection cell, temperature control, optical detection, data display and host software in a single machine. This is not a superseded earlier concept — it is our picture of the final product, and it is the basis on which the current modules were designed: their interfaces, dimensions and specifications were all set to meet the requirements of the complete instrument.

The overall plan does include a display and host software. An instrument sitting in a classroom or a hospital ward has to show temperature and fluorescence readings without a computer attached to it, and it has to be able to export continuous data for trend analysis. Our order of work is to build the core functions first — temperature control, optics and the signal chain — and to make those three stable, repeatable and reproducible before layering display and host software on top. The current hardware revision covers the core functions, with the module interfaces already reserved for that later expansion.

Test Plan

CNC-machined test parts have been commissioned to replace the printed parts and to verify the parallelism and surface quality of the optical windows; the printed parts continue to be used for assembly and plug-interface checks.

Table 8. Tests the module has to pass, with the method and the criterion for each one.
TestMethod and criterion
Fit and assembly Insert / remove each plug 20 times and reassemble the vessel 5 times; check for play and reading drift (drift ≤ 5%)
Temperature accuracy Set 30 °C; after 20 min stabilisation measure inside the vessel bore with a calibrated thermometer; deviation ≤ ±0.5 °C
Temperature uniformity Measure at several heights in the vessel bore; difference ≤ 0.5 °C

Light-tightness and dark noise With the excitation source off under room lighting, acquire for 5 min; drift ≤ 1% of full scale
Optical alignment Insert and remove the plugs 10 times; spot position repeatability on a paper screen ≤ 0.5 mm
Background and fluorescence response Compare against blank medium; then compare HA-induced and uninduced yeast samples on the 528 nm channel
Repeatability Measure the same positive sample ≥ 5 times; RSD ≤ 5% (target, to be confirmed by data)
Co-culture compatibility Aerate and stir the medium in the cup for 30 min; observe foaming, evaporation and yeast viability, and record readout stability

Challenges and Lessons

  • Print settings and supports: the failed first print showed that print orientation and support placement must be considered at the design stage — long slender cones string badly.
  • Channel size: only after widening the optical channel to 8 mm did it become usable. That is the lower bound that still fits Ø10 mm filters, a lens assembly and alignment margin.
  • Detachable modules: making the optics plug-in moves the alignment problem from the assembled instrument into the plug itself. Once the plug is fixed, assembling the instrument becomes a repeatable insertion.
  • Co-culture cuts both ways: putting the yeast directly into the sampling liquid overlaps sampling time with expression time, but it also brings foaming, evaporation, contamination and a moving liquid surface into the optical path. Each of these has to be quantified during testing.
  • Enrichment is the bottleneck: conversations with experts made clear that target virus concentrations in air are extremely low, and that enrichment efficiency, more than detection sensitivity, limits the whole system. This is why we kept the cyclone sampling and liquid-enrichment route.
  • Validate before integrating: running temperature control and optics with a standard cuvette first keeps the uncertainty of a custom vessel out of optical debugging.

Future Work

  • Integrate the cyclone sampling front end with the heated block so that sampling, enrichment and detection close the loop in one cup, and quantify the enrichment efficiency.
  • Verify yeast viability and readout stability under co-culture, and fix the sampling flow rate, sampling duration and settling time.
  • Replace the PLA parts with the CNC parts once they arrive, and re-check window parallelism, surface quality and light-tightness.
  • Complete the first integrated test to establish detection limit, repeatability and response time.
  • Once the core functions are validated, add the data display and host software in line with the overall plan, and complete the full instrument specification.
