How PAGER antigen-gated receptors work

Nanobodies (Nb) specifically recognize and bind influenza HA antigen; through steric hindrance and conformational changes, they push the autoinhibitory peptide MT1 away from the receptor’s orthosteric binding site. Once the binding site is exposed, the small-molecule agonist DCZ can bind and activate the receptor. The activated receptor transmits signals downstream via the G protein-coupled pathway, ultimately initiating reporter gene expression driven by the FUS1 promoter.

How the PAGER receptor is designed to work. The yeast cell membrane is designed to carry an hM1Dq receptor whose extracellular recognition unit is an anti-H1N1 HA nanobody (Nb), while the MT1 inhibitor is designed to keep hM1Dq blocked whenever no antigen is present. When an influenza A (H1N1) HA antigen binds the nanobody, the inhibitor is designed to be displaced from the receptor; with DCZ holding hM1Dq ready, the receptor is designed to hand the signal to the humanised Gα protein (the Saccharomyces cerevisiae Gpa1 C-terminus, last five residues changed from KIGII to EYNLV) and then to the yeast MAPK cascade, where the FUS1 promoter drives the yEGFP or lacZ reporter. All of this is the intended design; no detection performance is claimed here.

Future Vision: From Modular Research to Shared Public Health Value

From Modular Research to Shared Public Health Value

EchoYeast’s contribution goes beyond the pursuit of a single biosensor. By sharing characterized reporter parts, yeast-engineering evidence, computational hypotheses, and the lessons from designs that did not yet work, we aim to give future teams a clearer starting point. Our long-term vision is to explore how modular living-cell sensing could support environmental monitoring—through open research, careful validation, and responsible design.

Our contribution today is a documented foundation for further research.

Our vision for tomorrow is more accessible, evidence-based environmental monitoring.

  1. 01

    Reusable Knowledge for Future Teams

    We contribute FUS1-based reporter parts, yeast-editing components, and documentation of Gpa1 C-terminal engineering. Together with construction records and characterization results, these resources provide starting points for future yeast-biosensor projects. Clearly distinguishing demonstrated results from unresolved questions helps others assess what can be reused and what still requires validation.

  2. 02

    A Future Role in Environmental Monitoring

    Our long-term goal is to explore whether yeast-based sensing could complement existing approaches to monitoring influenza-related environmental signals. Such a system would not replace clinical testing or determine whether an individual is infected. Any future application would require evidence of specificity, sensitivity, reliability, and performance in relevant sample conditions.

  3. 03

    Accessibility as a Design Goal

    Our temperature-controlled optical-readout prototype allows us to explore how biological sensing might be paired with accessible instrumentation. Affordability, ease of operation, maintenance, and local resource needs remain design priorities rather than demonstrated advantages. Their practical value must be assessed alongside biological performance.

  4. 04

    Open Development, Responsible Progress

    We aim to share not only successful modules, but also limitations—including unresolved full-length receptor expression and HA-dependent activation. Future work must address biological containment, sample handling, and the interpretation of results. By making both progress and uncertainty visible, EchoYeast seeks to support more transparent and responsible biosensor development.
