EchoYeast: a yeast-cell POCT biosensor

SZPU-China is a student team from Shenzhen Polytechnic University competing in iGEM 2026. We engineer the budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae BY4741 as a chassis to build EchoYeast — a programmable, low-cost, point-of-care biosensor for the rapid on-site detection of influenza A virus (IAV), based on the PAGER (Protein-fragment Assisted Interaction Reporter) antigen-gated receptor strategy.

When the sample contains IAV hemagglutinin (HA) antigen, a membrane-displayed anti-HA nanobody captures it; the resulting steric hindrance releases an autoinhibitory peptide from a humanized hM1Dq receptor, and — in the presence of the DCZ agonist — activates a downstream G-protein pathway that drives a FUS1 promoter to express yEGFP / lacZ reporters. The result is a direct "antigen binding → light emission" conversion.

Why influenza A, and why a rapid field test?

Influenza A virus (IAV) is a leading cause of seasonal epidemics and repeated public-health emergencies. The H1N1 subtype has caused multiple pandemics in history; its rapid mutation and respiratory transmission make early, low-cost field screening a critical gap in outbreak control. Existing clinical methods struggle to balance sensitivity, cost, throughput and portability:

  • qRT-PCR: the gold standard for sensitivity and specificity, but requires expensive qPCR instruments, a professional lab and long turnaround — hard to deploy in resource-limited regions.
  • ELISA: suitable for high-throughput screening, but lower sensitivity and laborious incubation/wash steps with high operator training demand.
  • Colloidal gold strips: simple and instrument-free, yet mostly qualitative and prone to false negatives at low viral load.
  • Cell culture / egg isolation: days to weeks, used mainly for research and strain identification, not field diagnosis.

Nanobodies (VHH): derived from the variable domain of camelid heavy-chain antibodies (~12–15 kDa), they are thermostable, soluble and easy to express in prokaryotic/eukaryotic systems — an ideal building block for a modular antigen-recognition unit.

Design strategy: PAGER rewires a humanized GPCR

We build an HA-recognition circuit in yeast based on the PAGER strategy. The design turns the yeast's native mating pheromone pathway into an "antigen-unlock → luminescence" detection platform: without HA antigen, the MT1 inhibitory peptide occupies the orthosteric site of the humanized hM1Dq receptor and the downstream G-protein cascade stays off; once viral HA is captured by the membrane-displayed anti-HA nanobody, steric hindrance physically displaces MT1, relieving autoinhibition so that DCZ can activate hM1Dq, which — through the Gpa1-Gαq chimeric protein — triggers the FUS1 promoter to drive yEGFP or lacZ.

  • Chassis: S. cerevisiae BY4741, CRISPR-Cas9 knockout of STE2, FAR1, Sst2 to lower background, release cell-cycle arrest and amplify signal.
  • Recognition: anti-H1N1 HA nanobody (VHH) guided and anchored to the yeast membrane via the α-factor signal peptide.
  • Signal module: humanized hM1Dq receptor and Gpa1-Gαq chimera (Gpa1 C-terminus KIGII → human Gαq EYNLV).
  • Output: dual-mode FUS1p-yEGFP (flow-cytometry quantification) and FUS1p-lacZ (X-Gal colorimetric readout).

Build & Test: a modular wet-lab workflow

Guided by the Design–Build–Test–Learn (DBTL) engineering cycle, the project decomposes a complex synthetic-biology goal into independently verifiable modules — from gene design and synthesis of the PAGER fusion protein, through E. coli plasmid amplification and restriction validation, yeast chassis CRISPR modification and homologous-recombination humanization, protein-expression verification, and finally functional induction and dual-mode signal detection of the biosensor.

  • Design: codon-optimize α-factor-(GGGS)₃-MT1-(GGGS)₃-anti-HA-TEVcs-hM1Dq for yeast, adding NdeI/BamHI sites.
  • Build: gene synthesis cloned into pGADT7; pML104-sgRNA plasmid and URA3-marked humanized Gpa1 integration cassette prepared.
  • Test: LiAc/PEG transformation into the modified chassis; Western Blot for membrane expression; DCZ-gradient induction read by flow cytometry and X-Gal staining.
  • Learn: iterate on sgRNA efficiency, inducer concentration and culture conditions from signal background, response amplitude and dose–response.

Safety and structural validation

The project strictly follows the iGEM Safety White Paper and the host institution's biosafety management. All genetic operations are performed in a standard molecular-biology lab using the BSL-1 model organism S. cerevisiae BY4741; virus-related work uses only inactivated viral components or pseudovirus systems — no live-virus release, environmental exposure or human-cell infection.

  • Biosafety level: food-grade yeast BY4741, BSL-1, non-pathogenic; team biosafety trained.
  • Genetic material: recognition element only expresses an HA-specific nanobody, no complete viral genome or replication-competent particles.
  • Waste management: culture, medium and consumables autoclaved at 121 °C for 30 min; restriction enzymes, DCZ and X-Gal handled per MSDS with gloves and goggles.
  • Structure-assisted design: homology modeling predicts the anti-HA nanobody / H1N1 HA interface to guide mutation design and affinity optimization.

Human Practices: from experts to the public

Through expert interviews, surveys and outreach, we continuously align the technical design with real social needs. Feedback from clinical testing, CDC and primary-care professionals helped us identify the pain points of existing tests in accessibility, cost and turnaround; outreach to secondary-school students showed that "visual, low-cost, instrument-free" is the public's most intuitive expectation of a biosensor. Together these inputs calibrate the project goal toward a "fast, deployable, modular broad-spectrum respiratory-pathogen surveillance platform."

Future vision: from H1N1 to a broad-spectrum platform

After the H1N1 proof of concept, the core value of the PAGER-Yeast platform is its modularity and replaceability: the membrane nanobody recognition unit can be rapidly iterated for different antigens while the downstream GPCR rewiring and dual-mode reporter stay generic. Combined with a low-cost portable fluorescence reader, the system can move from a lab prototype to a field-usable tool.

  • Modular recognition: keep the α-factor anchor + linker framework, swap only the VHH to cover H5, H7 and beyond (SARS-CoV-2, RSV).
  • Signal optimization: map fluorescence to viral load via DCZ concentration/time gradients for semi-quantitative detection.
  • Multi-scene use: primary-clinic screening, campus morning checks, port quarantine, home self-test.
  • Open & standard: submit promoters, reporters and signal modules to the iGEM Registry in compatible form; release reproducible protocols and plasmid maps.
