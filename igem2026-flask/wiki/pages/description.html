Executive summary: Rapid on-site testing for influenza A virus (IAV) runs into a clinical bottleneck: existing methods are too slow or too costly for field use. This project uses Saccharomyces cerevisiae BY4741 as the chassis and applies the PAGER programmable antigen-gated receptor technology to turn HA antigen recognition into a green fluorescent signal, building a low-cost, high-sensitivity point-of-care biosensor.

Background and Motivation

Problem and Impact

Influenza is a major global public-health threat, and fast, accurate on-site screening is central to controlling transmission. Current clinical tests have the following limitations:

  • qRT-PCR: High accuracy, but costly, dependent on specialised laboratory equipment and slow, so it is hard to deploy at the primary-care level or in the field.
  • ELISA: Suitable for high-throughput testing, but its sensitivity is lower, the incubation steps are involved, and it demands a controlled operating environment.
  • Colloidal gold test strip: Simple to operate and fast on site, but it gives only a qualitative read-out and can miss samples with a low viral load.

These methods cannot reconcile cost, sensitivity and portability at the same time, which makes a low-cost, highly sensitive, field-deployable detection platform necessary.

Project Goals

The core goal is to apply the PAGER (Protein-fragment Assisted Interaction Reporter) programmable antigen-gated receptor technology in S. cerevisiae, build a membrane-surface antigen-recognition fusion protein, and achieve rapid, visual detection of influenza A HA antigen.

When IAV HA antigen is present, nanobodies displayed on the yeast surface capture it. The resulting steric hindrance relieves receptor autoinhibition and, with the DCZ agonist present, activates the downstream G-protein pathway. The FUS1 promoter then drives yEGFP/lacZ expression, producing green fluorescence or a blue colour, so that antigen binding is converted directly into a measurable signal.

Why This Project

Yeast was chosen as the chassis for several reasons: BY4741 has a well-characterised genetic background, the molecular tools are mature, cultivation is simple and it is BSL-1, which suits scaled fermentation. The PAGER system decouples antigen recognition from receptor activation, so swapping the nanobody alone extends the sensor to other pathogens, which makes the design modular and extensible.

Executive summary: The PAGER strategy achieves antigen gating through a three-part chimera of MT1 inhibitory peptide, hM1Dq receptor and anti-HA nanobody: the pathway stays closed without HA, and HA relieves the steric block and activates the dual-mode reporter system.

Core Design: the PAGER Strategy

Antigen-Gated Receptor Principle

PAGER(Protein-fragment Assisted Interaction Reporter, protein-fragment assisted interaction reporter) is a programmable antigen-gated receptor strategy built on steric control. Its core is to combine an inhibitory peptide, an antigen-recognition nanobody and a G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) into a single membrane fusion protein.

Complete architecture: α-factor-(GGGS)₃-MT1-(GGGS)₃-anti-HA-TEVcs-hM1Dq. The flexible (GGGS)₃ linkers act as spacers so that the nanobody can reach through the yeast cell-wall pores and contact the viral particle.

1

Resting Closed State

Without IAV HA antigen, the MT1 inhibitory peptide keeps occupying the orthosteric site of the hM1Dq receptor, the downstream G-protein cascade is held in the closed state and no fluorescence is produced.

2

Antigen Recognition and Steric Unlocking

HA antigen binds the surface-displayed anti-HA nanobody, and the resulting steric bulk physically displaces the MT1 inhibitory peptide from the hM1Dq binding pocket, relieving autoinhibition.

→
3

DCZ Activation and Signal Output

The small-molecule agonist DCZ activates hM1Dq. The Gpa1–Gαq chimera triggers the MAPK pathway, and the FUS1 promoter drives yEGFP/lacZ expression, producing green fluorescence or a blue colour.

Dual-Mode Reporter System

Two reporter systems, fluorescent and colorimetric, were built to cover quantitative analysis, high-throughput screening and naked-eye read-out:

Fluorescent Reporter

pFUS1-yEGFP

yEGFP serves as the green fluorescent reporter. Fluorescence intensity can be quantified by flow cytometry, giving high sensitivity and a wide dynamic range for precise quantification and high-throughput screening.

Colorimetric Reporter

pFUS1-lacZ

lacZ encodes β-galactosidase, which turns blue after staining with the X-Gal substrate. This allows qualitative visual read-out without expensive instruments, which suits resource-limited field settings.

Both systems share the FUS1 promoter and differ only in the reporter gene, so the sensor response can be verified through the same pathway, together or independently.

Chassis Engineering Targets

To remove endogenous signal interference, amplify the pathway response and restore human GPCR coupling, the BY4741 chassis was engineered as follows:

STE2
Encodes the endogenous yeast α-factor receptor
Engineering strategy: A single-target sgRNA directs CRISPR-Cas9 to introduce a frameshift, giving a full gene knockout.
Purpose: Removes the endogenous α-factor receptor and lowers background noise.
FAR1
Cell-cycle arrest protein downstream of the pheromone pathway
Engineering strategy: A single-target sgRNA directs CRISPR-Cas9 to introduce a frameshift, giving a full gene knockout.
Purpose: Relieves mating-induced cell-cycle arrest so that growth can be followed in real time.
Sst2
Negative regulator of G-protein signalling
Engineering strategy: A single-target sgRNA directs CRISPR-Cas9 to introduce a frameshift, giving a full gene knockout.
Purpose: Removes the negative regulator of G protein and amplifies the fluorescent signal.

In addition, Gpa1 is not knocked out. Instead, homologous recombination is used for a five-residue substitution at the C terminus: the native KIGII is replaced by the human Gαq sequence EYNLV, so that the humanized hM1Dq receptor couples efficiently to the yeast MAPK pathway.

Executive summary: The project follows a four-stage pipeline — gene synthesis, amplification and verification in E. coli, chassis engineering in yeast, and protein expression with functional testing — to build the complete biosensor in a modular way.

Experimental Design and Modular Construction

The experimental work is divided into four functional modules. Each is developed independently and then integrated step by step into a complete yeast biosensor that responds to HA antigen.

01

Chassis Cell Engineering — Removing Endogenous Interference and Amplifying the Signal

Objective: Knock out STE2, FAR1 and Sst2 in BY4741 with CRISPR-Cas9 to rebuild a zero-background, high-sensitivity signalling host.

The pML104 vector expresses Cas9 and a single-target sgRNA. The construct was transformed into yeast by LiAc/PEG, positive clones were selected on SD-Ura plates and verified by colony PCR and Sanger sequencing. Current status: in progress; the sgRNA vector is being rebuilt after a restriction digest failed.

02

Reporter System Construction — FUS1-Driven Dual-Mode Output

Objective: Build the pFUS1-yEGFP and pFUS1-lacZ reporter plasmids so that the signal can be both visualized and quantified.

Using pESC-HIS as the backbone, the FUS1 promoter was fused to either yEGFP or lacZ. Once the reporter strain receives the upstream G-protein signal, transcription starts: yEGFP is used for flow-cytometric quantification and lacZ for qualitative X-Gal staining.

03

Gpa1-Gα Humanization — Restoring Human GPCR Coupling

Objective: Use the homologous recombination machinery of S. cerevisiae to replace the C-terminal KIGII of Gpa1 with the human Gαq EYNLV sequence, coupling hM1Dq to the yeast MAPK pathway.

The linear integration cassette consists of a 500 bp upstream homology arm, the humanized Gpa1 sequence, a URA3 selection cassette and a 500 bp downstream homology arm. BY4741 is a ura3 auxotroph, so positive integrants can be selected on SD-Ura plates and confirmed by colony PCR and Sanger sequencing. Current status: waiting for the chassis knockouts to be completed.

04

PAGER Recognition Fusion Protein — Assembling and Expressing the Core Sensor Element

Objective: Synthesize the PAGER fusion gene, amplify it in E. coli and transform it into yeast, then verify membrane expression and antigen responsiveness.

The fusion gene was codon-optimized for S. cerevisiae, flanked with NdeI and BamHI sites, synthesized in full and cloned into pGADT7 (plasmid ABX63942). Transformation into Top10 competent cells, plasmid extraction and double-digest verification confirmed the construct: four positive clones gave plasmid concentrations of 408.45 / 404.85 / 297.15 / 355.90 ng/μL with A260/A280 ≈ 1.9, and the band pattern matched the expected sizes (7939 bp backbone, 2063 bp insert). Next, the construct will be transformed into yeast by LiAc/PEG, protein expression will be checked by Western blot, and induction conditions will be optimized with a DCZ gradient (10 nM, 100 nM, 1 μM).

Executive summary: The project offers innovations on three levels — technology, chassis and platform — and carries social value in lowering public-health testing costs, empowering primary care and establishing a general pathogen-detection platform.

Innovation and Social Value

Three Innovations

Technology: the PAGER system is extended for the first time to the detection of a large viral antigen in yeast, with steric hindrance used as the antigen-gated receptor switch.
Chassis: knocking out STE2, FAR1 and Sst2 together with the humanized Gpa1–Gαq chimera markedly lowers background noise and improves signal sensitivity.
Platform: the modular design makes the nanobody recognition unit swappable, so the sensor can be extended quickly to COVID-19, RSV, IAV variants and other respiratory pathogens.

Social Value and Outlook

Lowering public-health testing costs: S. cerevisiae can be produced by large-scale fermentation and does not need expensive PCR instruments, which suits population-level screening.

Empowering primary care: the workflow does not depend on standard laboratory conditions, so it fits rapid screening in community clinics, schools and border ports.

Industrializing synthetic biology: a modular general-purpose detection platform supports national infectious-disease surveillance.

Longer-term directions: optimize the signal output for semi-quantitative viral-load measurement and pair it with a portable fluorescence reader; swap the surface nanobody to cover COVID-19, RSV, IAV variants and other respiratory pathogens; and ultimately develop a standardized test kit for commercial use.
