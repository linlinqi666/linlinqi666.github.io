Wet-Lab Contributions: Reusable Parts for Yeast Engineering

Our wet-lab contribution is a set of documented genetic components and characterization results that future teams can use when engineering yeast signaling systems. The parts cover pheromone-responsive reporting, FAR1/SST2 editing and GPA1 C-terminal modification. Their sequences and intended roles are indexed on our Parts page, while construction and experimental evidence are documented on our Engineering and Results pages. Parts · Engineering · Results

1. Pheromone-responsive reporter parts

Featured parts: BBa_26J53MF4 — FUS1 promoter · BBa_26D8D1SG — FUS1–yEGFP reporter fragment

We provide a defined 435-bp FUS1 promoter fragment and a 1,152-bp composite fragment combining this promoter with the yEGFP coding sequence. The promoter is recorded separately from the reporter, allowing future teams to reuse the regulatory element or the assembled fluorescence readout according to their own designs. Parts

We characterized the reporter in BY4741 using α-factor induction, fluorescence imaging, time-course measurements and growth assays. These experiments provide a reference for evaluating downstream yeast pheromone-pathway output and comparing engineered chassis strains. Results

Value for future teams: a characterized reporter starting point for yeast signaling studies. The composite is a reporter fragment, not a complete standalone expression system; a suitable vector and transcriptional termination context must be supplied. Its α-factor characterization does not establish HA detection. Parts · Design

2. FAR1/SST2 editing components

Featured composite parts: BBa_26IKMSKO — FAR1 sgRNA expression cassette · BBa_26YVX4QT — SST2 sgRNA expression cassette

Matching repair templates: BBa_26HN2W3G — FAR1 repair template · BBa_262SR0ED — SST2 repair template

We provide target-specific sgRNA expression cassettes and matching repair templates for FAR1 and SST2 editing in BY4741. Each cassette combines a yeast RNA polymerase III promoter, a target-specific spacer, the SpCas9 sgRNA scaffold and a terminator. The repair templates are designed to introduce frameshift-producing four-base-pair deletions. Parts

Following an unsuccessful initial construction round, we revised guide assembly and repair-template design. Colony screening and Sanger sequencing subsequently identified the intended FAR1 and SST2 edits, and growth and fluorescence measurements characterized the resulting chassis under α-factor induction. Engineering · Results

Value for future teams: matched targeting and repair components, together with a documented editing workflow and genotype-to-phenotype evaluation. These components require Cas9 and the appropriate donor and host context; the sgRNA cassette alone is not a complete knockout system. Parts · Engineering

3. A Gpa1 C-terminal engineering module

Featured basic part: BBa_26EG2LOR — Gαq-derived EYNLV sequence with stop codon

Associated composite: BBa_266K1VDV — GPA1 C-terminal humanization cassette

BBa_26EG2LOR is an 18-bp module encoding the Gαq-derived C-terminal sequence EYNLV followed by a stop codon. We used this sequence in a GPA1-editing design intended to replace the native Gpa1 C-terminal pentapeptide KIGII. It is a replacement segment, not a complete G-protein coding sequence. Parts

After revising the donor-construction strategy to overlap-extension fusion PCR, we obtained strains 12 and 17 with the expected target-region sequence. The humanized chassis was further characterized through α-factor-induced morphology, growth and FUS1–yEGFP fluorescence measurements, which showed attenuated native pheromone responses. Engineering · Results

Value for future teams: a defined replacement module and a documented starting point for studying yeast G-protein interfaces. The evidence supports the edited-chassis phenotype; it does not independently establish hM1Dq coupling or attribute every observed change solely to the 18-bp replacement. Results

Reusable construction and troubleshooting records

Alongside these parts, we document unsuccessful construction attempts and the design changes that followed, including revised sgRNA assembly, repair-template redesign and the switch from a multi-round donor-construction route to overlap-extension fusion PCR. These records give future teams practical alternatives to evaluate rather than only presenting the final successful constructs. Engineering

From Successful Engineering to Functional Validation

Our experimental work addresses two specific questions: how can the native signaling regulation of yeast be reliably modified, and how can we determine whether precise engineering of the G-protein interface changes cellular signaling behavior?

Translating Gene Editing into Verifiable Phenotypes

Obtaining the target strain was not the endpoint of our work. During the editing of FAR1 and SST2, our initial construction attempt failed to yield the desired strains, prompting us to revise our construction strategy. We ultimately confirmed the intended genetic modifications by sequencing. The resulting strains carried a 4-bp deletion in each target gene, providing clear sequence-level evidence for subsequent validation.

More importantly, we combined genotype verification with growth and fluorescence characterization under α-factor induction. Comparing the wild-type, single-knockout, and double-knockout strains allowed us to determine whether the effects of gene deletion extended beyond the DNA level to cellular growth and reporter output. Our results showed that the double knockout altered the growth response to stimulation and produced higher reporter fluorescence under the tested conditions. These findings support the functional characterization of the engineered chassis beyond successful strain construction alone.

Refining Construction Methods to Ensure Sequence Accuracy

Unlike the double-knockout strategy, GPA1 engineering required precise replacement of five amino acids at the C-terminus of the protein. Our initial strategy, which combined Gibson Assembly with multiple rounds of PCR, resulted in sequence errors in the donor fragment. We subsequently adopted overlap-extension fusion PCR to reorganize the homology arms and selection cassette. This revised strategy yielded strains 12 and 17, whose target sequences were confirmed by sequencing. The modification replaced the native C-terminal pentapeptide KIGII with EYNLV, corresponding to the human Gαq C-terminal sequence.

The value of this work lies not only in obtaining the correctly modified strains, but also in documenting why the construction strategy was changed, how the alternative approach addressed the initial problem, and how the final sequence was verified. These details provide a practical reference for researchers undertaking precise genomic modifications in yeast, offering more transferable information than a positive PCR result alone.

Connecting Sequence Modifications to Cellular Behavior

Both engineering strategies required us to address a question beyond successful construction: did the modified cells exhibit the expected functional changes? For the double-knockout strategy, growth curves and fluorescence output were used to examine the relationship between signaling responses and cellular state. For GPA1 humanization, we further investigated growth and morphological changes under α-factor induction.

The current results indicate that GPA1-humanized yeast exhibits altered phenotypes associated with native pheromone stimulation. However, these changes do not by themselves demonstrate that a heterologous GPCR has successfully coupled to the downstream yeast signaling pathway. In addition, the descriptions of fluorescence responses in different sections of our Wiki are inconsistent. The direction and magnitude of this response must therefore be determined by re-examining the original data before a definitive conclusion can be drawn. Distinguishing confirmed findings from observations that remain unresolved is an important part of the experimental knowledge we make available to future researchers.

Reusable Design Resources for Yeast Signaling Chassis Engineering

We organized two core aspects of our yeast chassis engineering—native signaling regulation and G-protein input-interface reprogramming—into biological parts that can be independently accessed, adapted, combined, and further validated. These resources provide concrete sequence references and starting points for future yeast signaling engineering projects.

A Directly Accessible Genetic Engineering Strategy for Yeast

We submitted the FAR1 and SST2 sgRNA expression cassettes (BBa_26IKMSKO and BBa_26YVX4QT, respectively), together with their corresponding repair templates (BBa_26HN2W3G and BBa_262SR0ED), to the iGEM Registry. Each repair template incorporates a 4-bp deletion and a PAM-site modification designed to disrupt the target gene and reduce the likelihood of repeated recognition by the same sgRNA following editing.

These parts provide sequence-level design references for researchers seeking to modify cell-cycle responses or negative regulation of G-protein signaling in yeast. By consulting the published sequences and construct information, users can assess their suitability after checking the target locus, PAM sequence, and homology regions in their own strain, rather than designing the entire editing system from scratch.

A Sequence-Level Starting Point for Cross-Species Receptor Coupling

We submitted the GPA1 C-terminal humanization cassette with a URA3 selection marker (BBa_266K1VDV), as well as a basic part encoding the Gαq-derived pentapeptide EYNLV followed by a stop codon (BBa_26EG2LOR). The composite cassette contains upstream and downstream homology arms, the target replacement sequence, and the URA3 selection module, providing a defined construct for targeted modification of the yeast GPA1 locus.

The target sequences in strains 12 and 17 were verified, providing experimental evidence that the intended sequence replacement was achieved. For teams seeking to connect heterologous GPCRs to yeast signaling networks, this construct offers a concrete starting point for further design and comparison. Nevertheless, successful sequence replacement does not establish effective coupling of the human receptor to the yeast signaling pathway; downstream signal transmission must be validated independently.

Model

Computational from end to end, which makes it the part another team can run without a lab. Three outputs: a docking pipeline that narrows a candidate panel, an atomic-level account of how the gating peptides sit on their receptors, and the files that reproduce both.

Seventeen candidates, one lead

Screening a nanobody panel costs reagent and weeks. We replaced the first pass with two docking stages, a rigid ClusPro sweep over the whole panel and HADDOCK 2.5 semi-flexible refinement on what survived, and added a specificity index so that H1N1-selective binders separate from cross-reactive ones instead of everything ranking by docking score. Seventeen candidates became one lead, R1aB6, with no binding assay run.

A team with a panel and no structures can repeat this on predicted models and order a shortlist instead of a library. The specificity index is the part worth taking, because without it the ranking collapses back into "best score wins", which is how cross-reactive binders get ordered. No binding assay has confirmed the ranking.

Both gating interfaces

An inhibitory-peptide gate only works if blocking the outside leaves the inside intact. We tested that on both systems: hM1Dq with the MT1 peptide and Ste2 with MT1_5, each membrane-aligned before docking, refined in HADDOCK, then run through CHARMM-GUI membrane building, 100 ns GROMACS dynamics, SPICE interface analysis and gmx_MMPBSA. Both come out the same way, with the peptide in the orthosteric site and the intracellular G-protein interface left free.

That is a check on an assumption, not a demonstration of gating in cells, and it is written as the former. The workflow is the reusable part: it transfers to any receptor and peptide pair, including ones with no experimental structure.

One hotspot, one hypothesis

One number did most of the work here. Per-residue decomposition of the R1aB6–HA complex puts ARG-B:31 at −5.46 kcal/mol, well clear of its neighbours, which turns into a single experiment: make R31A and measure it by SPR or ELISA.

That is what a model is good for, choosing the next experiment rather than illustrating the last one. A team with a docked complex and no assay capacity can run the same decomposition and come out with one residue to mutate, which is far cheaper than scanning an interface. The mutation is designed and the measurement is planned; neither has been run.

The pipeline as files

A pipeline published without its inputs is a figure, not a tool. HADDOCK and ClusPro inputs, parameter files and analysis scripts are kept with the work, and the 9VH4 complex is re-docked through the same pipeline as a positive control so the numbers have something to sit against.

The docking results also became an interactive card game, which is the form we use when the audience is not a modelling audience. Both are on the Model page; the game runs there rather than as a download.

Human Practices Contributions

Preface to Influenza A in the Air. After our social practice, we recognised that many important questions about influenza A virus in the air — how it is transmitted, how long it can persist in air and on surfaces, how environmental conditions affect it, and what different detection signals can actually establish — draw on several fields and are dispersed across the literature. We therefore read the relevant studies and organised what we learned into this Influenza A in the Air contribution, bringing together the evidence, key data, study conditions and references needed to examine these questions. The document does not present new experimental findings; it structures existing research into a continuous, accessible and traceable resource that can help readers develop an overall understanding of airborne transmission, environmental persistence, detection and control of influenza A. In this way, it provides a reference for learning, discussion and project development, reduces duplicated searching, and makes the material available for further use, checking and improvement within the iGEM community.

The discussion format: APiC question cards

Our Human Practices work produced a second, less obvious contribution: a format for peer discussion. At the APiC project exchange we replaced the usual sequence of project presentations with structured question cards built around iGEM award tracks. Each card directed discussion through five dimensions — value positioning, design rationale, analysis of previous projects, critical self-evaluation, and future optimisation — so that teams spent their time examining why a design was appropriate rather than describing what it aimed to do.

The format proved reusable because it does not depend on shared subject matter. Teams working on different biological questions can use the same cards, because the questions are about reasoning rather than about pathogens. The cards and the process are documented so that future organisers can adapt them to other award tracks, exchange events and review sessions.

The workshop record: making experimental experience shareable

The third contribution came out of an online yeast workshop that brought together CAU-China, NEFU-China, SZPU-China, Keystone, NKU and SHSID. The discussion covered transformation efficiency, gene knockout, selection stability, reporter leakage, membrane-protein expression and localisation, product formats and biosafety. Much of what made it useful was not the methods themselves but the troubleshooting attached to them: which failure modes were recognised, which controls distinguished them, and what remained unresolved.

Two lessons from that discussion are recorded in the workshop write-up. The first is a methodological caution: a mutation at a target site does not by itself establish that Cas9 cleavage caused it, so controls and independent genotype verification are needed before a knockout is claimed. The second is that methods shared by other teams should be preserved with their attribution — CAU-China's combination of fluorescently tagged receptors, FM4-64 membrane staining and confocal microscopy, and the alginate microsphere and freeze-dried colour-card formats discussed by Keystone and NKU, are recorded as theirs. None of the methods discussed originated with us; the value of the record lies in the exchange itself and in keeping each method attached to the team that described it.

What we learned about sharing knowledge

Three things became clear across these activities. Structured questions produce better exchanges than presentations, because they force specificity. Documented troubleshooting is more valuable than documented success, because most teams fail in similar ways. And a contribution is strongest when it preserves attribution, because that is what allows the next team to build on it rather than repeat it.
