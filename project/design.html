Background

Why detect influenza A virus?

Influenza A virus (IAV) poses persistent global public-health threats. According to WHO estimates, seasonal influenza causes approximately 1 billion infections, 3-5 million cases of severe illness, and 290,000-650,000 respiratory deaths worldwide each year, especially among children, the elderly and immunocompromised populations. Influenza A virus is highly variable: its surface hemagglutinin (HA) protein continuously escapes existing population immunity through antigenic drift and antigenic shift1.

Traditional influenza surveillance mainly relies on “patient-centered sampling”: swabs from symptomatic patients are collected for laboratory testing. This workflow only reflects infections after people develop clinical symptoms. Once patients show fever or cough, viruses may already spread widely in shared indoor spaces. Early warning before large-scale transmission is hard to achieve by human-centered monitoring alone2.

Accumulated evidence confirms that aerosol transmission is a critical route for IAV spread in crowded indoor environments, such as schools, hospitals, nursing homes, subway stations and dormitories3. Virus-carrying fine aerosol particles can stay suspended in air for hours, infecting people via inhalation even without close contact with patients4.

Air-borne pathogen monitoring brings unique public-health value:

  1. Pre-symptom early warning: Detect viral hazards in indoor air before mass symptomatic cases emerge, enabling proactive ventilation, disinfection and crowd-flow management, rather than only responding after infections happen.
  2. Identify transmission hot-spots: Distinguish high-risk locations inside buildings (classrooms, wards, waiting halls) and guide targeted prevention measures.
  3. Complement existing surveillance system: Current national influenza networks focus on clinical patients. Air monitoring adds environmental dimension data to support epidemic trend assessment for public-health decision-making.
  4. Protect high-density congregate settings: Schools and nursing homes suffer heavy influenza-related absences and severe-case burdens. Real-time air risk evaluation helps reduce cluster-outbreak risks for vulnerable groups.

Challenges in Airborne Influenza A Virus Monitoring

Detecting IAV in air faces intrinsic physical difficulty: viral particles are highly diluted in ambient air, with extremely low actual concentration. Today's mainstream paradigm for airborne-virus detection is exemplified by bio-aerosol sampling combined with quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (qRT-PCR), the gold-standard laboratory method. This workflow is divided into two discrete steps: bio-aerosol samples are first collected on-site and then transported back to a central laboratory for qRT-PCR analysis. No mature, low-cost on-site POCT solution exists for air-borne IAV surveillance5.

Solution

Overall Design

To address this Challenge, our team designed an engineered yeast whole-cell biosensor that aims to achieve low-cost, high-sensitivity, field-deployable detection of airborne influenza A virus. Our system adopts the PAGER (Protein-fragment Assisted GPCR Expression Reporter) programmable antigen-gated receptor strategy, adapted from a published synthetic GPCR framework1. In the presence of the hemagglutinin (HA) antigen from influenza A virus, the receptor is de-repressed, thereby triggering the downstream yeast MAPK mating pathway and activating reporter gene expression to produce a fluorescent readout.

Our project follows a modular design strategy:

  1. Chassis Cell Engineering. FAR1, and SST2 are knocked out to remove endogenous interference, prevent cell cycle arrest, and enhance signal sensitivity2,3.
  2. Humanized Gpa1-Gαq chimeric G protein. The yeast Gpa1 C-terminus is replaced with the human Gαq C-terminus to couple hM1Dq to the yeast MAPK pathway4.
  3. PAGER antigen-gated fusion receptor protein design for HA recognition. hM1Dq is a human M1 DREADD receptor that can be activated by DCZ1. Anti-IAV HA nanobody (VHH) specifically binds H1N1 or H3N2 HA5. MT1 muscarinic toxin acts as an auto-inhibitory peptide that occupies the orthosteric site of the hM1Dq receptor. Antigen binding relieves this inhibition via steric hindrance1.
  4. FUS1-driven reporter output module. The yeast FUS1 promoter drives yEGFP or MEL1 expression, producing green fluorescence or blue color as the output signal6.

Once these four modules are assembled and validated, the sensor will be further interfaced with an air sampler (e.g., a cyclone sampler or impactor) to collect airborne particles into a liquid medium. The collected liquid is then applied to the yeast sensor, enabling airborne influenza A virus detection.

Module 1: Chassis Cell Engineering — Knockout of FAR1 and SST2

Design Purpose

Wild-type Saccharomyces cerevisiae BY4741 possesses a native mating pheromone response MAPK pathway. Two endogenous factors interfere with our synthetic human-origin GPCR (hM1Dq) signal transmission:

Cell-cycle arrest: pheromone stimulation triggers Far1-mediated G1 arrest, preventing continuous proliferation and long-term real-time monitoring2.

Negative regulation: Sst2 acts as a negative regulator of G-protein signaling, dampening the signal amplitude of the GPCR–MAPK cascade3.

To re-wire the native pathway for our synthetic sensor, we perform targeted gene editing to remove growth arrest and enhance signal sensitivity, creating a chassis better suited for downstream human GPCR coupling.

Gene Knockout Design

We use the CRISPR-Cas9 system to introduce frameshift mutations in two target genes, FAR1 and SST2, in BY4741. sgRNAs targeting the coding sequences of these genes are cloned into the pML104 vector, which carries both the Cas9 expression cassette and the sgRNA expression cassette. After transformation, Cas9 is guided by the sgRNAs to cleave the target loci, and the resulting double-strand breaks are repaired by non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), frequently generating frameshift mutations that disrupt gene function. Correctly edited clones will be verified by colony PCR and Sanger sequencing.

Module 2: Humanized G-Protein Engineering — Gpa1-Gαq Chimera

Design Purpose

Native yeast Gpa1 (the Gα subunit) cannot recognize the human-derived hM1Dq GPCR. To couple hM1Dq to the yeast intracellular MAPK pathway, we need to engineer the Gα subunit so that it can be recognized and activated by hM1Dq. Literature indicates that the C-terminal 5 amino acids of the Gα protein determine GPCR–Gα recognition specificity. Replacing the C-terminus of yeast Gpa1 with the corresponding human Gαq sequence is sufficient to change its receptor coupling specificity while preserving its ability to activate the yeast downstream pathway4.

Gpa1–Gαq chimeric protein design

Native yeast Gpa1 (Gα subunit) cannot recognize human-derived hM1Dq GPCR. Literature indicates that C-terminal 5 amino acids of Gα protein determine GPCR–Gα recognition specificity4.

  • Wild-type yeast Gpa1 C-terminal 5 aa: “KIGII”
  • Replace with human Gαq C-terminal 5 aa: “EYNLV”

The chimeric Gpa1-Gαq retains the yeast Gpa1 backbone, which is capable of activating the yeast Gβγ (Ste4/Ste18) and downstream Ste5/Ste7/Fus3 MAPK cascade, but its C-terminus is now compatible with human hM1Dq.

To humanize the C-terminus of yeast Gpa1, a donor cassette containing the upstream homology arm (Homology-U), the URA3 selectable marker, and the downstream homology arm (Homology-D) was designed, with the humanized 5-amino-acid sequence EYNLV followed by a stop codon placed between the two homology arms. This cassette was introduced into S. cerevisiae by transformation, where homologous recombination directed its integration into the native GPA1 locus on Chromosome VIII (region 116,000–111,500 bp; NCBI RefSeq: GCF_000146045.2), replacing the original C-terminal motif KIGII. Correctly edited clones were selected on SD-Ura medium and verified by colony PCR and Sanger sequencing, yielding the Gpa1-Gαq chimera for functional coupling with hM1Dq.

Module 3: PAGER Antigen‑Gated Fusion Receptor Protein Design

Design Purpose

We adopt PAGER synthetic GPCR “antigen-gated de-inhibition” logic. In absence of target HA antigen, receptor stays self-inhibited with very low background signal. When influenza A HA antigen appears, antigen-nanobody binding relieves self-inhibition, enabling downstream signal activation upon DCZ small-molecule agonist stimulation1.

Our full-length fusion protein domain arrangement (N-terminal to C-terminal): MT1 auto-inhibitory peptide – anti-HA nanobody – TEV protease cleavage site – ALFA tag – hM1Dq receptor.

Each domain function:

  1. MT1 auto-inhibitory toxin peptide: Constitutive suppressor domain for hM1Dq. Without HA antigen, MT1 binds hM1Dq and locks receptor in inactive state to minimize background leakage signal1.
  2. anti-HA nanobody: Recognition element, high-specific affinity binding to hemagglutinin HA of influenza A virus. When HA binds nanobody, steric hindrance displaces MT1 peptide away from hM1Dq, releasing receptor inhibition5.
  3. TEV protease cleavage site: Built-in positive-control switch. TEV protease cleavage can artificially remove MT1 inhibition, to validate whether downstream signaling cascade works normally.
  4. hM1Dq human-derived GPCR: Core receptor. After de-repression, small-molecule agonist DCZ can activate hM1Dq, which transduces intracellular signals through our Gpa1-Gαq chimera to trigger the yeast MAPK pathway1.

Module 4: Reporter Output Module: FUS1 Promoter Reporter System

After the MAPK signaling cascade is activated, a readable output signal is required for biosensor readout. We selected the native yeast FUS1 promoter, which is natively triggered by the yeast mating-pheromone MAPK cascade. Once the upstream receptor is activated, FUS1 initiates transcription of the downstream reporter gene6.

We built two independent single-reporter plasmids for parallel testing. One produces fluorescent output (yEGFP), the other produces colorimetric output (MEL1 /α-galactosidase with X-α-Gal substrate). By comparing signal intensity, background noise, dynamic range and experimental operability between the two systems, we aim to identify the more suitable readout strategy for our yeast biosensor7.

Plasmid backbone: pESC-HIS. Two constructed reporter plasmids:

pESC-HIS-FUS1-yEGFP: Fluorescent output, quantitative measurement via fluorescence detector.

pESC-HIS-FUS1-MEL1: Colorimetric output, chromogenic reaction with X-α-Gal substrate.

Experimental goal: Evaluate and select the optimal read-out modality for our biosensor from fluorescence or colorimetric output.

Safety & Design Considerations

  1. Chassis biosafety: We use non-pathogenic laboratory-standard Saccharomyces cerevisiae BY4741, a widely used biosafety-level-1 microbial chassis with no pathogenic risk. We adopt auxotrophic selection markers on our expression plasmids; engineered yeast strains can only survive on culture media supplemented with corresponding deficient amino acids. This imposes a biological containment barrier: the engineered yeast cannot stably proliferate in natural environments lacking the required nutrients, minimizing the risk of uncontrolled environmental escape.
  2. Recognition element: anti-HA nanobody and hM1Dq receptor are heterologous protein expression; no virulence-related genes are introduced. No replication-competent viral sequences are used in our constructs.
  3. Control logic: Self-inhibition design greatly reduces constitutive leakage activation, lowering false-positive risk of sensor and improving the signal-to-noise ratio of our biosensor.
  4. Modularity design: Our whole system is modularized. Only replacing the nanobody domain can change detection target, supporting future adaptation to other respiratory pathogens such as RSV or coronavirus.
