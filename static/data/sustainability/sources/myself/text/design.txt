 Background

 Why detect influenza A virus?

 Influenza A virus (IAV) poses persistent global public-health threats. According to WHO estimates,
 seasonal influenza causes approximately 1 billion infections, 3-5 million cases of severe illness, and
 290,000-650,000 respiratory deaths worldwide each year, especially among children, the elderly and
 immunocompromised populations. Influenza A virus is highly variable: its surface hemagglutinin (HA)
 protein continuously escapes existing population immunity through antigenic drift and antigenic shift 1 .

 Figure 1. Transmission routes of influenza A virus and typical high-risk indoor scenarios for airborne IAV exposure 

 Traditional influenza surveillance mainly relies on &ldquo;patient-centered sampling&rdquo;: swabs from
 symptomatic patients are collected for laboratory testing. This workflow only reflects infections after
 people develop clinical symptoms. Once patients show fever or cough, viruses may already spread widely in
 shared indoor spaces. Early warning before large-scale transmission is hard to achieve by human-centered
 monitoring alone 2 .

 Accumulated evidence confirms that aerosol transmission is a critical route for IAV spread in crowded
 indoor environments, such as schools, hospitals, nursing homes, subway stations and dormitories 3 .
 Virus-carrying fine aerosol particles can stay suspended in air for hours, infecting people via inhalation
 even without close contact with patients 4 .

 Air-borne pathogen monitoring brings unique public-health value:

 Pre-symptom early warning: Detect viral hazards in indoor air before mass symptomatic
 cases emerge, enabling proactive ventilation, disinfection and crowd-flow management, rather than only
 responding after infections happen.

 Identify transmission hot-spots: Distinguish high-risk locations inside buildings
 (classrooms, wards, waiting halls) and guide targeted prevention measures.

 Complement existing surveillance system: Current national influenza networks focus on
 clinical patients. Air monitoring adds environmental dimension data to support epidemic trend assessment
 for public-health decision-making.

 Protect high-density congregate settings: Schools and nursing homes suffer heavy
 influenza-related absences and severe-case burdens. Real-time air risk evaluation helps reduce
 cluster-outbreak risks for vulnerable groups.

 Challenges in Airborne Influenza A Virus Monitoring

 Detecting IAV in air faces intrinsic physical difficulty: viral particles are highly diluted in ambient
 air, with extremely low actual concentration. Today's mainstream paradigm for airborne-virus detection is
 exemplified by bio-aerosol sampling combined with quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (qRT-PCR), the
 gold-standard laboratory method. This workflow is divided into two discrete steps: bio-aerosol samples are
 first collected on-site and then transported back to a central laboratory for qRT-PCR analysis. No mature,
 low-cost on-site POCT solution exists for air-borne IAV surveillance 5 .

 Solution

 Overall Design

 To address this Challenge, our team designed an engineered yeast whole-cell biosensor that aims to achieve
 low-cost, high-sensitivity, field-deployable detection of airborne influenza A virus. Our system adopts the
 PAGER (Protein-fragment Assisted GPCR Expression Reporter) programmable antigen-gated receptor strategy,
 adapted from a published synthetic GPCR framework 1 . In the presence of the hemagglutinin (HA)
 antigen from influenza A virus, the receptor is de-repressed, thereby triggering the downstream yeast MAPK
 mating pathway and activating reporter gene expression to produce a fluorescent readout.

 Figure 2. Modular Design of the Engineered Yeast Biosensor. The chassis is rewired for GPCR-MAPK coupling; the PAGER receptor enables HA-gated de-repression; the FUS1-driven cassette provides reporter output. 

 Our project follows a modular design strategy:

 Chassis Cell Engineering. FAR1, and SST2 are knocked out to remove endogenous
 interference, prevent cell cycle arrest, and enhance signal sensitivity 2,3 .

 Humanized Gpa1-G&alpha;q chimeric G protein. The yeast Gpa1 C-terminus is replaced
 with the human G&alpha;q C-terminus to couple hM1Dq to the yeast MAPK pathway 4 .

 PAGER antigen-gated fusion receptor protein design for HA recognition. hM1Dq is a
 human M1 DREADD receptor that can be activated by DCZ 1 . Anti-IAV HA nanobody (VHH)
 specifically binds H1N1 or H3N2 HA 5 . MT1 muscarinic toxin acts as an auto-inhibitory peptide
 that occupies the orthosteric site of the hM1Dq receptor. Antigen binding relieves this inhibition via
 steric hindrance 1 .

 FUS1-driven reporter output module. The yeast FUS1 promoter drives yEGFP or MEL1
 expression, producing green fluorescence or blue color as the output signal 6 .

 Once these four modules are assembled and validated, the sensor will be further interfaced with an air
 sampler (e.g., a cyclone sampler or impactor) to collect airborne particles into a liquid medium. The
 collected liquid is then applied to the yeast sensor, enabling airborne influenza A virus detection.

 Module 1: Chassis Cell Engineering — Knockout of FAR1 and SST2

 Design Purpose

 Wild-type Saccharomyces cerevisiae BY4741 possesses a native mating pheromone response MAPK
 pathway. Two endogenous factors interfere with our synthetic human-origin GPCR (hM1Dq) signal transmission:

 Cell-cycle arrest: pheromone stimulation triggers Far1-mediated G1 arrest, preventing
 continuous proliferation and long-term real-time monitoring 2 .

 Negative regulation: Sst2 acts as a negative regulator of G-protein signaling, dampening
 the signal amplitude of the GPCR&ndash;MAPK cascade 3 .

 To re-wire the native pathway for our synthetic sensor, we perform targeted gene editing to remove growth
 arrest and enhance signal sensitivity, creating a chassis better suited for downstream human GPCR coupling.

 Gene Knockout Design

 We use the CRISPR-Cas9 system to introduce frameshift mutations in two target genes, FAR1 and SST2, in
 BY4741. sgRNAs targeting the coding sequences of these genes are cloned into the pML104 vector, which
 carries both the Cas9 expression cassette and the sgRNA expression cassette. After transformation, Cas9 is
 guided by the sgRNAs to cleave the target loci, and the resulting double-strand breaks are repaired by
 non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), frequently generating frameshift mutations that disrupt gene function.
 Correctly edited clones will be verified by colony PCR and Sanger sequencing.

 Figure 3. Signal Transduction Mechanism and Chassis Optimization of the Engineered Yeast Biosensor 

 Module 2: Humanized G-Protein Engineering — Gpa1-G&alpha;q Chimera

 Design Purpose

 Native yeast Gpa1 (the G&alpha; subunit) cannot recognize the human-derived hM1Dq GPCR. To couple hM1Dq to
 the yeast intracellular MAPK pathway, we need to engineer the G&alpha; subunit so that it can be recognized
 and activated by hM1Dq. Literature indicates that the C-terminal 5 amino acids of the G&alpha; protein
 determine GPCR&ndash;G&alpha; recognition specificity. Replacing the C-terminus of yeast Gpa1 with the
 corresponding human G&alpha;q sequence is sufficient to change its receptor coupling specificity while
 preserving its ability to activate the yeast downstream pathway 4 .

 Gpa1&ndash;G&alpha;q chimeric protein design

 Native yeast Gpa1 (G&alpha; subunit) cannot recognize human-derived hM1Dq GPCR. Literature indicates that
 C-terminal 5 amino acids of G&alpha; protein determine GPCR&ndash;G&alpha; recognition specificity 4 .

 Wild-type yeast Gpa1 C-terminal 5 aa: &ldquo;KIGII&rdquo;

 Replace with human G&alpha;q C-terminal 5 aa: &ldquo;EYNLV&rdquo;

 The chimeric Gpa1-G&alpha;q retains the yeast Gpa1 backbone, which is capable of activating the yeast
 G&beta;&gamma; (Ste4/Ste18) and downstream Ste5/Ste7/Fus3 MAPK cascade, but its C-terminus is now
 compatible with human hM1Dq.

 To humanize the C-terminus of yeast Gpa1, a donor cassette containing the upstream homology arm
 (Homology-U), the URA3 selectable marker, and the downstream homology arm (Homology-D) was designed, with
 the humanized 5-amino-acid sequence EYNLV followed by a stop codon placed between the two homology arms.
 This cassette was introduced into S. cerevisiae by transformation, where homologous recombination
 directed its integration into the native GPA1 locus on Chromosome VIII (region 116,000&ndash;111,500 bp;
 NCBI RefSeq: GCF_000146045.2), replacing the original C-terminal motif KIGII. Correctly edited clones were
 selected on SD-Ura medium and verified by colony PCR and Sanger sequencing, yielding the Gpa1-G&alpha;q
 chimera for functional coupling with hM1Dq.

 Figure 4. Schematic of GPA1 Locus Modification and Humanized Sequence Integration via Homologous Recombination 

 Module 3: PAGER Antigen&#8209;Gated Fusion Receptor Protein Design

 Design Purpose

 We adopt PAGER synthetic GPCR &ldquo;antigen-gated de-inhibition&rdquo; logic. In absence of target HA
 antigen, receptor stays self-inhibited with very low background signal. When influenza A HA antigen appears,
 antigen-nanobody binding relieves self-inhibition, enabling downstream signal activation upon DCZ
 small-molecule agonist stimulation 1 .

 Our full-length fusion protein domain arrangement (N-terminal to C-terminal): MT1 auto-inhibitory
 peptide &ndash; anti-HA nanobody &ndash; TEV protease cleavage site &ndash; ALFA tag &ndash; hM1Dq receptor.

 Each domain function:

 MT1 auto-inhibitory toxin peptide: Constitutive suppressor domain for hM1Dq. Without
 HA antigen, MT1 binds hM1Dq and locks receptor in inactive state to minimize background leakage
 signal 1 .

 anti-HA nanobody: Recognition element, high-specific affinity binding to hemagglutinin
 HA of influenza A virus. When HA binds nanobody, steric hindrance displaces MT1 peptide away from hM1Dq,
 releasing receptor inhibition 5 .

 TEV protease cleavage site: Built-in positive-control switch. TEV protease cleavage
 can artificially remove MT1 inhibition, to validate whether downstream signaling cascade works normally.

 hM1Dq human-derived GPCR: Core receptor. After de-repression, small-molecule agonist
 DCZ can activate hM1Dq, which transduces intracellular signals through our Gpa1-G&alpha;q chimera to
 trigger the yeast MAPK pathway 1 .

 Figure 5. Schematic Representation of the Structural Composition and Signal Transduction of the Engineered PAGER Receptor 

 Module 4: Reporter Output Module: FUS1 Promoter Reporter System

 After the MAPK signaling cascade is activated, a readable output signal is required for biosensor readout.
 We selected the native yeast FUS1 promoter, which is natively triggered by the yeast mating-pheromone MAPK
 cascade. Once the upstream receptor is activated, FUS1 initiates transcription of the downstream reporter
 gene 6 .

 We built two independent single-reporter plasmids for parallel testing. One produces fluorescent output
 (yEGFP), the other produces colorimetric output (MEL1 /&alpha;-galactosidase with X-&alpha;-Gal
 substrate). By comparing signal intensity, background noise, dynamic range and experimental operability
 between the two systems, we aim to identify the more suitable readout strategy for our yeast biosensor 7 .

 Plasmid backbone: pESC-HIS. Two constructed reporter plasmids:

 pESC-HIS-FUS1-yEGFP: Fluorescent output, quantitative measurement via fluorescence detector.

 pESC-HIS-FUS1-MEL1: Colorimetric output, chromogenic reaction with X-&alpha;-Gal substrate.

 Experimental goal: Evaluate and select the optimal read-out modality for our biosensor
 from fluorescence or colorimetric output.

 Safety &amp; Design Considerations

 Chassis biosafety: In this study, the non-pathogenic laboratory standard strain
 Saccharomyces cerevisiae BY4741 was selected as the chassis cell. This strain belongs to
 Biosafety Level 1 (BSL-1) microorganisms and poses no pathogenic risk. In terms of biosafety design, we
 exploit its auxotrophic properties to construct a biological containment barrier, thereby reducing the
 risk of uncontrolled dissemination of the engineered strain in natural environments. BY4741 is naturally
 auxotrophic for histidine (His), leucine (Leu), methionine (Met), and uracil (Ura). The PAGER system, the
 reporter system, and the plasmids for Gpa1 humanization and gene knockout constructed in this study carry
 LEU, HIS, and URA selection markers, respectively, which complement the corresponding auxotrophies. To
 ensure biosafety, we further designed a marker-removal strategy: the URA marker will be removed by
 5-fluoroorotic acid (5-FOA) counter-selection, so that the engineered strain will ultimately maintain a
 double auxotrophy for uracil and methionine. This double-auxotrophy mechanism is designed to render the
 strain unable to proliferate effectively outside the specific supplemented medium, thereby effectively
 reducing the risk of environmental release.

 Recognition element: anti-HA nanobody and hM1Dq receptor are heterologous protein
 expression; no virulence-related genes are introduced. No replication-competent viral sequences are used
 in our constructs.

 Control logic: Self-inhibition design greatly reduces constitutive leakage activation,
 lowering false-positive risk of sensor and improving the signal-to-noise ratio of our biosensor.

 Modularity design: Our whole system is modularized. Only replacing the nanobody domain
 can change detection target, supporting future adaptation to other respiratory pathogens such as RSV or
 coronavirus.
