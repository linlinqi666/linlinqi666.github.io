 01 
 Overview

 Influenza A is a seasonal influenza disease that usually breaks out during specific periods each year. Although the outbreak period is limited, influenza A imposes an extremely heavy global disease burden during each epidemic season. According to World Health Organization (WHO) reports, each year there are about 3–5 million severe cases and 290,000–650,000 influenza-related respiratory deaths. Influenza A does not originate in humans but comes from poultry, and influenza transmitted from poultry to humans has another name, “avian influenza”, such as “H5N1”. Avian influenza itself can only infect a small number of humans and cannot continue to spread from person to person, but it has undergone a terrifying change in pigs. This is because the respiratory tract of pigs carries both avian α-2,3 and human α-2,6 receptors. And by an unfortunate coincidence, when the same pig is infected with both avian influenza and an already circulating human influenza, the eight RNA segments of the two viruses reshuffle and reassort, directly producing a completely new strain. The new strain may at once acquire the ability to recognize human receptors, to replicate in human cells and to spread from person to person. Influenza A epidemics have a distinct cyclical character: after the peak of an epidemic, case numbers fall rapidly and public vigilance gradually fades; but by the next epidemic season, the mutated influenza A virus returns, once again threatening human health.

 To improve influenza A prevention and control, we hope to use synthetic biology to capture and detect influenza A virus, and this is how our project, Echo Yeast, came about. To build a device that is practically applicable and socially valuable, we cannot confine ourselves to theoretical reasoning and experimental validation inside the laboratory; we need to work with people outside, improving our project through the questions and suggestions of experts so that it matches the real conditions and needs of society. We first mapped out our project's stakeholders, including pulmonology clinicians, frontline nurses, researchers and institutional staff. The suggestions they raised provided a realistic basis for our project concept, guided the optimization of our approach, offered ideas for model construction, and refined the device's application scenarios and many practical details. It is precisely with the help of experts from all walks of life that our project design is able to fit actual user needs more closely.

 02 
 Action Research Framework: Letting Human Practices Actually Drive the Project

 Action Research · Turning expert feedback into engineering decisions

 In iGEM, Human Practices is not material patched together after the competition; it is a process that runs through the entire project and continuously revises the team's judgments. We adopt action research, proposed by Kurt Lewin in 1946, as the theoretical framework for organizing our human practices, turning exchanges with external experts and partners into an iterable, traceable loop.

 What is action research

 Action research is a spiral-cycle method of “researching while doing, improving through research”, with four stages: diagnosis — clarifying the real problem; planning — designing the intervention; action — implementing it; observation and reflection — evaluating the results and feeding them back into the next round. It emphasizes collaboration between practitioners (us) and researchers (the experts), letting external feedback enter decision-making directly.

 Two-level cycles: project level and interview level

 We apply action research at two levels. At the project level: every round of human practices pushes the project from “an idea” toward “a more reliable design”, and all DBTL cycles revolve around “requirement — design — verification — learning”, with the ultimate goal of delivering a working live-virus detector within the August–November cycle. At the interview level: each expert interview also follows “diagnosis → planning → action → reflection” — first finding out what the other party cares about, then asking targeted questions, then acting on the advice, and finally reviewing what actually changed the project.

 How the focus shifts across iterations

 As the cycles advance, the team's focus keeps shifting: the first round asks “is this useful, and where is it used” (requirement validation); the second turns to “how do we make it accurate and stable” (technical feasibility); the third starts asking “how do we make people willing to use it and able to afford it” (implementation and outreach); later rounds return to “which feedback is still not closed, and what evidence is missing” (filling the gaps). Each shift marks the completion of one action-research cycle and the start of the next.

 Making reflection traceable

 To make the “feedback → decision” chain traceable, we compile the conclusions of every interview and every exchange into structured records: who the expert was, what questions were raised, how we responded, and which parts of the project changed as a result. These records are not just reporting material; they are the evidence chain of the project's self-correction, and they let us answer, clearly and in front of the judges, “how exactly did Human Practices change your project”.

 Industry · Shenzhen, Guangdong

 Wenjie Wang

 Director of the Medical Technology Department at a Grade II Class A hospital in Shenzhen

 The project's technical direction and its practical application positioning are still unclear, and the team lacks first-hand knowledge of real influenza A testing scenarios. 

 The team designed interview questions around mutation mechanisms, clinical testing, treatment windows, test-kit updates, application scenarios and the feasibility of self-testing. 

 Interviewed Dr. Wenjie Wang to learn about hospital testing logic, the use of specific antiviral drugs, test-kit updates and his suggestions on air monitoring. 

 We recognized the gap between laboratory models and real needs and decided to focus on the conserved HA stalk region, enclosed-space testing and proactive early-warning applications. 

 The team had largely settled on its technical route: use engineered yeast as the chassis, display nanobodies on its surface to recognize the conserved stalk region of influenza A virus HA, and build a biosensor that can capture airborne virus and output a detection signal. But a settled technical route does not mean the project is viable; we needed a frontline clinical laboratory expert to assess its practical applicability, and we wanted to talk directly with a clinical laboratory expert to understand the real pain points of influenza A testing in clinical settings, treatment strategies and prevention needs.

 Dr. Wenjie Wang pointed out that the existing IVD industry always acts only after the virus has broken out in the human body and reached the detection threshold, whereas our air-monitoring sensor could shift prevention and control from passive diagnosis and treatment to proactive prevention, issuing a warning before the virus enters the body — which has significant public health value. He also identified shortcomings in both the technical and the application side of our project:

 Air monitoring involves many variables, virus concentrations in open environments are low, and the sensor's effective range may be limited.

 The effective range and limiting conditions are still unclear. We need to test the effective measurement range, the size of the environment, the stage the virus is at and so on; otherwise it is difficult to define the real application boundary. (link to the hardware section)

 False-positive and specificity risks are prominent. The false-positive rate of self-test reagents can reach 50%–70%; if the sensor moves toward portable self-testing, specificity and threshold determination must be solved.

 The clinical need for subtyping is limited: hospitals focus on detecting whether the virus is positive or negative in order to guide treatment and do not distinguish subtypes; subtyping is announced by the CDC.

 Translation and standardization are insufficient: the project is still at the laboratory prototype stage, and production, cost, standardization and actual deployment still need to be addressed.

 Drawing on frontline experience, the doctor also advised us to focus on “detecting airborne virus” rather than distinguishing subtypes; to test the effective range first in enclosed spaces, controlling invariant factors before studying variables; to clarify limiting conditions such as the effective measurement range, the size of the environment and the stage of the virus; to refer to immunochromatography in developing portable self-testing while strictly controlling false positives; and to actively contact reagent manufacturers to promote translation of the results. In essence, these suggestions require us to fill four categories of data: sensitivity, specificity, environmental adaptability and application boundaries.

 The team turned the expert's suggestions into action: first, firmly choose the conserved HA stalk region to achieve broad-spectrum detection and de-emphasize subtype discrimination; second, prioritize the design of enclosed-space experiments and establish a variable-control plan; third, make specificity and false-positive control a focus of subsequent optimization; fourth, define the effective measurement range and environmental limits; and fifth, this interview moved the project from a technical concept toward a problem-driven one and made us keenly aware that a cutting-edge sensor, if it is truly to serve public health, requires not only innovation but also the ability to withstand the test of clinical scenarios, environmental variables and real-world use.

 Industry · Guangzhou, Guangdong

 Kai Fu

 MD in clinical medicine, Sun Yat-sen University

 Although the air-monitoring direction was recognized, questions remained about timeliness, virus concentration, cost and the boundaries of expansion. 

 Questions were designed around response speed, the reporter system, yeast survival, device cost, clinical scenarios and future expansion. 

 Interviewed Dr. Kai Fu, MD in clinical medicine at Sun Yat-sen University, to obtain advice on clinical feasibility, breath testing and grassroots promotion. 

 Confirmed that a single-person breath mode is superior, and that response time must be shortened, costs controlled and expansion into lung cancer approached cautiously. 

 The project had already focused on the conserved HA stalk region, de-emphasized subtype discrimination and begun exploring air monitoring. However, questions such as the yeast sensor's response time, reporter system, cost, yeast lifespan, comparison with existing technologies, whether ambient air or human breath is superior, and whether it could be extended to influenza B or lung cancer still needed to be calibrated.

 During the interview, Dr. Kai Fu endorsed the concept of “using an organic organism to detect another organic organism”, which he considered uniquely valuable in synthetic biology. He particularly noted that the breath of people with respiratory or lung infections carries virus particles expelled with aerosols, and that the device would detect such people more effectively. But he also cautioned that the yeast signaling pathway takes about 1–2 hours from receiving a signal to expressing fluorescence; if it takes too long, the device is not timely enough. Airborne virus in ambient air is diluted, and influenza A can survive in air for only a few hours, whereas virus in the human body is more concentrated and easier to detect.

 Based on these judgments, Dr. Kai Fu's advice centered on four directions. First, prioritize a single-person breath mode, supported by an enrichment device, with environmental sampling as a supplement. Second, optimize the signaling pathway to shorten response time; fluorescence requires photoelectric conversion and software readout, while blue–white screening can be judged with the naked eye but its expression time is still to be determined. Third, control hardware costs — an active breath device is expected to come down to around 10 yuan, which is what makes grassroots promotion feasible. Fourth, conduct cross-disciplinary interviews with experts in respiratory medicine, biomedical engineering, virus research and other fields, defer lung cancer testing and focus on respiratory viruses such as influenza A; expansion could follow if highly specific breath markers are identified in future. In addition, he reminded us to clarify the yeast's lifespan and replacement plan: activity peaks 2–3 days after revival and can be maintained for 1–2 weeks.

 Dr. Kai Fu advised us to focus subsequently on shortening detection response time and controlling hardware cost, and to assess clinical compatibility prudently when expanding the technology. The team thereby understood the possibility of moving from “ambient air monitoring” to “single-person breath testing”. What we had gained: the technical direction was recognized, single-person breath testing is superior to environmental sampling, and low false positives, high sensitivity and ease of operation constitute differentiating advantages. What still needed to be supplemented: the expression time of blue–white screening, the cost of the complete device, long-term yeast survival and replacement plans, quantitative specificity and sensitivity data, a systematic comparison with PCR/test strips, and validation in grassroots applications. This interview advanced the project's clinical feasibility and reminded us that a cutting-edge technology must answer four questions at once: is it accurate, is it fast, is it affordable, and where is it used?

 Science and academia · iGEM mentor

 Xinyuan Qiu

 Synthetic biology expert

 Our team uses the yeast [[i]]Saccharomyces cerevisiae[[/i]] ([[i]]S. cerevisiae[[/i]]) as the chassis and builds an airborne influenza A biosensor based on the PAGERs system; we have completed the construction of some genetic elements, but have repeatedly hit bottlenecks in experimental design, chassis engineering and iGEM judging strategy. We therefore invited Xinyuan Qiu to guide our current experimental plan and our iGEM work. The expert pointed out the problems in our project, clarified the key validations currently missing, and offered valuable advice.

 Chassis and route: validate the premise first, then talk about simplification 

 The expert raised a key question that we had long overlooked: “Can an intact virus pass through the cell wall and reach the cell membrane?” He pointed out that this is the physical premise for whether the device can work: the yeast cell wall is thick, and there is currently no evidence that intact virus particles can penetrate the cell wall and bind the antibodies on hM1Dq; if validation fails, we should resolutely change the chassis (for example, to Xenopus oocytes) rather than continue investing in complex modifications. Based on this judgment, the expert's core advice was “the simpler the better”: we had previously been stuck on issues such as knocking out endogenous interfering genes and validating the gating system, whereas the expert preferred modifying the yeast's own RTK (receptor tyrosine kinase) system, replacing only the extracellular domain with an influenza A surface antibody protein, and verifying downstream phosphorylation by Western blot. On the reporter system, he offered two routes: adjust the number of PREs or switch to a weaker promoter to reduce leakage; at the same time, molecular-level evidence must be added, using a Western blot of MAPK phosphorylation to prove signal activation rather than relying only on indirect judgment from growth phenotype. The expert also stressed that “strong antigen–antibody specificity” does not equal “high detection sensitivity”: sensitivity must be defined as the number of detectable virus particles per unit volume, and our team precisely lacks the quantitative basis for “the minimum detectable number of particles per unit volume”. For serial knockout of multiple genes, he suggested switching to Golden Gate cloning for plasmid construction to improve the success rate of cloning short sgRNA fragments.

 Modeling: use it smartly, not exhaustively 

 We had hesitated over whether to build a full-chain ODE (ordinary differential equation) model from antigen to GFP, even though most parameters could only be obtained by fitting. The expert considered it acceptable in review to approximate a multi-step reaction with a single Hill function, but the parameters must come from experimental fitting or literature citations and must never be set arbitrarily; the derivation of that approximation must also be written clearly on the wiki. The real value of a model lies in first validating it by matching curves to experimental data, and then using parameter sensitivity analysis to trace back the most sensitive steps in order to guide experiments. Regarding our treating PAGERs as a cascade of “antigen recognition → release of MT1 inhibition → GPCR activation” and trying to encapsulate it with a three-state gate, the expert judged that an ODE description of the input–output relationship and dynamic response is more appropriate than three-state gate logic; mature algorithms already exist for sgRNA, so there is no need to spend effort on over-engineered forms such as Fourier transforms. The expert particularly reminded us that reviewers care about whether the model solves experimental problems, not whether the formalism is fancy; rather than stacking modules, it is better to make the model capable of predicting the whole “response–recovery” process.

 HP and the competition: necessity first, and the loop must be real 

 The expert pointed out that we must think about the “why” and build a complete project background and rationale. He also mentioned that expert statements and outreach cannot replace factual evidence and that “appeals to authority” should be avoided; we should use epidemiological and literature data to demonstrate the value of “airborne transmission detection” and the irreplaceability of the “yeast chassis”. Human Practices is not about quantity or formality; it focuses more on whether the practice actually influenced the project and drove improvement — for example, interview feedback at the hardware level must be translated into design improvements and be validated. The expert's strategy for moving forward was clear: the immediate priority is to produce a functional receptor that can recognize influenza A antigen and to complete the first “simple and clear” DBTL (Design-Build-Test-Learn) cycle before iterating; he also suggested studying the judging handbook and contributing to the community by submitting new parts or education projects.

 Three shifts brought about by the interview 

 (1) Necessity first: the epidemiological basis for airborne transmission detection, the irreplaceability of the yeast chassis and the differentiating advantages over existing technologies are all essentially questions of “why the project exists”, and none of them currently has supporting data.

 (2) Review and reflection: we had focused our energy on humanizing the GPCR while neglecting the premise of “whether the virus can pass through the cell wall”; simplified routes such as RTK that “change only the extracellular domain” deserve repeated self-scrutiny in subsequent decisions.

 (3) Data are the only language of argument: the background cannot stop at verbal endorsement by experts, model parameters cannot be set by experience, and sensitivity must have quantitative metrics. The expert also affirmed the value of the innovative idea of transplanting PAGERs into yeast.

 After the interview, we raised project background research and chassis/receptor feasibility validation to the highest priority, and on that basis continue to iterate and optimize the project.

 Healthcare frontline · Hangzhou, Zhejiang

 Juan Luo

 ICU frontline nurse, Grade III Class A hospital, Hangzhou

 Working in an intensive care unit, she performs high-frequency procedures such as ventilator management and sputum suction for intubated or tracheostomized patients, giving her a frontline view of hospital air management, protective procedures and nosocomial infection control.

 Existing air disinfection in hospitals cannot fully inactivate aerosolized virus; high-risk procedures such as sputum suction require graded protection. The hardest part of prevention and control is visitors who are infectious without knowing it. If entrances and wards could display airborne virus readings in real time and grade them as low / medium / high risk, it would ease families' concerns; miniaturizing the device and integrating it into a ventilator's exhalation valve would enable per-patient monitoring.

 Hospitals rank equipment quality first and operation must not be cumbersome; hospitals also carry drug-resistant organisms such as Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae, so swapping the nanobody could extend the yeast sensor to multiple pathogens. Public hospitals, however, must procure equipment through open tender, and using it for diagnosis would require formal certification.

 After the interview we folded ventilator-valve integration and graded result display into the application design, and listed formal certification, tendering procedures, regulatory communication and modular multi-pathogen extension as follow-up directions. Note that the device has not entered any hospital system; the relevant approvals and certifications are still outstanding, not accomplished results.

 Policy and public health · Shenzhen, Guangdong

 Shisong Fang

 Deputy Director of the Pathogen Institute, Shenzhen Center for Disease Control and Prevention

 The project had settled on a technical route for influenza A detection, but the rigor of the topic selection, the choice of target genes, the bottleneck in air sampling, the registration pathway and the methods of scientific validation remained unanswered. 

 Interview Shisong Fang of the Pathogen Institute of the Shenzhen CDC, consulting on influenza typing, the rigor of the topic, detection technology design, air sampling, implementation and registration, and research methods. 

 Present the project and gather the expert's feedback on virus fundamentals, target-gene and antigen design, air collection, registration and translation, the surveillance system, and research for the competition. 

 We confirmed that testing for influenza A alone carries a risk of missed detection and that dual testing for influenza A and B should be included; target genes should preferentially be conserved regions; air sampling is the bottleneck of the whole chain; registration needs to distinguish research use from clinical use; and research must rely on real samples and statistical validation, while also requiring detailed investigation of influenza A's background and other details. 

 Although the project's technical route was settled, from the perspective of the CDC and public health we wanted to test the rigor of the topic selection and technical design. Our thinking was to focus on influenza A first, achieve influenza A recognition as a first step, and then consider subsequent expansion. We therefore interviewed the expert Shisong Fang; from the perspective of a government agency for influenza surveillance and laboratory verification, we wanted a CDC expert to review whether the idea of “focusing on influenza A recognition first and expanding step by step” holds up, and to point out regulatory issues in the technical design and later implementation that are easily overlooked.

 Shisong Fang began with the rigor of the topic selection, reminding us that testing for influenza A alone carries a risk of missed detection, that the clinical mainstream is dual testing for influenza A and B, and that the project presentation must face up to this shortcoming. On technical design, he recommended using conserved genes such as M/NP/NS for typing, designing primers with reference to sequences from the past 3–5 years and using BLAST to screen conserved fragments of about 200 bp; for antigen expression he preferred the conserved HA neck region, and suggested referring to the mosaic algorithm to cover circulating strains. He also stressed that a new project must be compared side by side with mature methods such as rapid antigen tests and fluorescence PCR across multiple dimensions, not only highlighting its advantages.

 Regarding airborne virus detection, the expert affirmed its innovative value and recommended crowded places such as hospital emergency departments, metro stations and schools, but advised against ICUs. He pointed out that the core difficulty lies in air-sample collection: virus particles are small, retention by filter membranes is limited, and collection protocols for bacteria and viruses are not interchangeable, so the choice of collector, wet versus dry collection, and membrane and concentration processes must be studied in depth; if the front end cannot capture the virus, no matter how advanced the back-end detection is, it is useless. On sensitivity, airborne viral loads are generally low, which places extremely high demands on enrichment and detection.

 On implementation, the expert distinguished two pathways — research equipment and clinical medical devices: research use requires no stringent registration, but the results have no clinical diagnostic validity; entering a hospital requires a Class III medical device registration certificate from the National Medical Products Administration, at least three clinical trial sites, and performance comparison against mature reagents. On research methodology, he advised obtaining real clinical samples, processing data with statistical methods, analyzing advantages and disadvantages objectively, and communicating more with industry experts rather than following textbooks blindly.

 At the end of the interview, the expert and the team agreed on follow-up work: the team would go back and improve the background research on influenza viruses and detection technologies; advance experimental validation with real samples and refine performance metrics and statistical analysis; subsequently engage medical-device industry experts to discuss device design, evaluate the pros and cons of the plan and determine the R&D steps; and stay in contact with the CDC expert whenever questions arose, with the expert also willing to visit the school later to review the project.

 From the perspective of public health and the CDC, this interview provided criteria different from those of clinical end users and industrial translation. On topic selection, we must face up to the risk of missed detection when testing only for influenza A and include dual testing for influenza A + B in subsequent planning; technically, prioritizing conserved target genes, choosing the HA neck for antigen expression and referring to the mosaic algorithm gave clear guidance for molecular design; on air sampling, the expert identified front-end capture as the bottleneck of the chain, requiring focused study of collectors, membranes and concentration processes; on implementation, the registration requirements differ between research equipment and clinical medical devices, so the goal must be clarified as early as possible; and in research methodology, real samples, statistical validation, side-by-side comparison and reproducibility became the basic principles for our subsequent experiments and reporting. The expert also said he would be available for contact at any time and was willing to come to the school to review the project, which provided important support for its continued progress.

 Industry and engineering · Shenzhen, Guangdong

 Rongkang Deng

 Manager at a biotechnology company in Shenzhen

 The project's device concept is clear, but the core bottleneck of airborne-virus enrichment has not yet been solved, and we lack experience in hardware engineering, R&D processes, component selection and competition delivery strategy. 

 Interview Deng, a medical-device hardware development engineer, consulting on the device plan, enrichment technology, R&D process, intellectual property and competition delivery. 

 Report the device concept and working mode, and gather Mr. Deng's feedback on enrichment approaches, R&D processes, competition strategy and industry practice. 

 Confirmed that airborne-virus enrichment is the greatest difficulty, that during the competition we should prioritize the principle block diagram and a partial demonstration unit rather than pursuing a complete prototype, and that the defense must show a thorough grasp of the principles to avoid being judged as fabricating results. 

 The project plans to develop a device that can collect influenza A virus from ambient air, enrich it, complete detection and issue a risk warning, targeting enclosed spaces such as schools and cruise ships. But we lack experience in hardware engineering, and in particular are unclear about airborne-virus enrichment, component selection, R&D processes and competition delivery. We therefore asked Mr. Deng, from the perspective of medical-device development, to help us judge what is realistic and what the competition stage should aim for.

 Mr. Deng first helped us organize our thinking: the detection chemistry and optics are actually manageable, and the real bottleneck is the capture and liquid-phase enrichment of virus in the air. Virus particles are extremely small, so if air is bubbled directly into the reaction liquid, most of the virus escapes as bubbles and dissolves with difficulty, and after prolonged bubbling the liquid also evaporates. He proposed two approaches: first, vortex agitation, so that air and liquid make full swirling contact; second, membrane filtration, with multiple membrane layers — the upper layer retaining bacteria and the lower nanomembrane retaining viruses, which are then washed off with eluent. The trouble with membrane filtration is that viruses easily pass through the membrane and high pressure causes clogging, which can be alleviated with pulsed pressurization or shaking. He also mentioned that exosome enrichment from urine follows a similar logic: it can be done in the lab, but in engineering it runs into practical problems such as time, clogging and impurities.

 On timing, Mr. Deng felt that producing a complete prototype in two or three months is unrealistic. He suggested first producing the appearance drawing, the working-principle block diagram and the module-level schematic diagrams to explain the logic clearly, and also making a partial demonstration unit of the core function rather than a fully connected machine. But he particularly reminded us that the team must thoroughly master the whole plan and must not present drawings while being unable to explain the principles, as that would be treated as fabricating results.

 He also laid out a fairly clear line for the R&D process: first write the Product Technical Requirements, setting out clearly everything that can be quantified — sampling duration, air-pump flow rate, liquid volume, temperature and humidity, false-positive/false-negative metrics and validation methods — while checking industry standards and similar devices; then determine the technical route, first building a simple simulation device to verify whether the virus can enter the liquid phase and how efficient the enrichment is, without rushing to build the complete machine; then split hardware and software, with the lower-level controller handling the air pump, solenoid valves and temperature control, and the host computer providing the human–machine interface, defining the communication protocol properly; then build a prototype and iterate through testing to find the balance between performance and cost. Since the device involves live virus, internal inactivation plans and safety validation must also be considered in advance.

 On tools and intellectual property, he suggested learning programming in a targeted way around subtasks rather than studying broadly; AI can be used, but key parameters and regulations must be checked against the original sources. On innovation, the vast majority of products are not original from scratch: one can refer to air purification equipment and enrichment devices in published papers, adapting parameters on an existing basis and prioritizing reuse of mature modules. On patents, the situation is relatively relaxed for students, but once commercialized, freedom-to-operate searches and design-arounds are required, and one must not directly copy or disassemble a competitor's product. Regarding yeast recognizing HA protein, he said he had not investigated commercial products of the same kind; fragment-level research might exist in laboratories, but complete devices are rare, and we would need to search the literature ourselves.

 Finally, he suggested that we prioritize producing the Product Technical Requirements document, then use it as the basis for the appearance, structure and principle block diagrams as competition materials; drawing and refining the plan can be discussed further later.

 This interview made the team realize that the main difficulty in building a complete device lies in airborne-virus enrichment. Subsequently we will prioritize completing the Product Technical Requirements document, clarifying the required specifications of the device we are building; based on the technical requirements, draw the appearance drawing, the working-principle block diagram and the module-level schematic diagrams as competition materials; and at the same time produce a partial demonstration unit of the core function. On component selection, we will prioritize reusing mature submodules, referring to air purification equipment and published enrichment devices and making adaptive adjustments rather than developing everything ourselves; on patents, we will carry out freedom-to-operate searches and design-arounds. For defense preparation, team members will thoroughly master the logic of the whole plan to ensure they can answer the judges' questions. Mr. Deng also said that further drawing and plan refinement can be discussed and coordinated later, providing continuing support for the project's hardware progress.

But we are not sure whether these tools are applicable to nanobody–protein systems, nor whether the result analysis is professional enough and able to withstand reviewers' follow-up questions. We therefore asked Mr. Liu to review our methodology and validation workflow.

 Mr. Liu first pointed out that the online docking website the team currently uses may not be designed specifically for protein–protein or antibody–antigen docking, and that the scope of application of its algorithm needs to be verified; nanobodies have a special structure, unlike Y-shaped IgGs, so general protein docking algorithms may not apply. He stressed that the fact that a website produces a result does not mean its methodology supports our system: we must read the original literature and confirm whether the sampling algorithm covers protein–protein docking and whether it is applicable to nanobodies.

 On modeling and structural preprocessing, homology modeling tools such as Swiss-Model are unreliable when template homology is below 25%, and he suggested switching to AlphaFold 3.0, whose predictions are more accurate and come with confidence scores; after modeling, a Ramachandran plot assessment is needed, requiring more than 95% of residues to fall in allowed regions; HA structures downloaded from the PDB need to have water, metal ions and salt ions removed, and hydrogen atoms added with charge balance.

 On result analysis and validation, presenting only the total binding energy is not enough; MM/GBSA free-energy decomposition should be performed, analyzing the contribution of each residue to verify whether the key interactions are concentrated in the CDR region; for visualization, heat maps and PyMOL are recommended to show binding details. Docking results represent only a rigid or semi-flexible state, so molecular dynamics simulation of at least 100 ns must be added to verify the stability of the complex; ultimately, SPR or MST wet-lab experiments should verify the actual affinity, forming a complete loop of “modeling–docking–analysis–MD–wet lab”. As for tools, industrial-grade software such as Rosetta and Boltz is authoritative but has a high deployment threshold; at this stage we can continue using online tools, but must confirm that the algorithm is public, has original literature and is widely cited by peers; PLIP can be used for interaction analysis. AI-assisted computation is acceptable, but the method must come from an open-source, peer-validated path.

 This interview made the team realize that molecular docking is not finished once a result is produced — the methodology itself must withstand scrutiny. Subsequently we will use AlphaFold 3.0 to remodel the 16 nanobodies and add Ramachandran plot assessments; preprocess the HA protein by removing ions, adding hydrogen and balancing charges; verify the original literature and scope of application of the existing docking website; perform MM/GBSA free-energy decomposition and produce residue-contribution heat maps; add molecular dynamics simulation to verify the stability of the complex; and ultimately close the loop with SPR/MST wet-lab experiments.  At this South China iGEM meetup, the team had face-to-face exchanges with several iGEM teams from South China and gained a great deal from project sharing, academic discussion and mock review.  Project presentation and mock review

 At the meetup, teams from different places could share their projects. Our team presented the influenza A air-detection project on the big screen, explaining the overall design logic from the yeast chassis and signal recognition to the reporter system. Other teams raised questions about low airborne virus concentrations, yeast sensitivity, reporter-system leakage and the differences from traditional detection methods. The mock review session that followed let us experience the pace of an iGEM defense in advance; some of our answers were not polished enough, but precisely for that reason they exposed the weak points in our project presentation. We recorded these questions and pieces of feedback one by one and, after the meeting, compiled them into a list for subsequent improvement. Looking at the questions collected, they mainly fell into four categories: at the experimental level, yeast response time, signaling pathway validation and virus concentration thresholds; at the mechanical and cost level, model calculations, cost control and yeast replacement; at the application-scenario level, where the device would be installed, data coverage in large scenarios and the feasibility of home self-testing; and other aspects such as the advantages over existing detection technologies, the project's actual function, whether similar research exists and biosafety approval. These questions basically covered the key links of the project from experiments to implementation.

 Parallel exchanges and interaction

 Besides the formal exchanges, the team visited other teams' project displays and learned about their design ideas and experimental arrangements in environmental detection, therapeutics and materials. Different teams had distinctive approaches to modeling, hardware and human practices, which provided us with plenty of reference points. The games and interactive sessions arranged at the meetup allowed teams to communicate progress and difficulties in a relaxed atmosphere, and some experience that would not normally be written into a wiki was shared during those exchanges.

 What this exchange meant for the project

 This meetup showed us where we fall short and clarified the direction for improvement. The team will put the suggestions received into practice in experiments and presentations, continue to refine the project, and prepare for follow-up work. These questions and contrasts from many teams complement our expert interviews, and together they complete the “feedback → decision” chain.

 APiC (Asia-Pacific iGEM Community Conference) was the first Asia-Pacific iGEM team conference, jointly hosted by the Southern University of Science and Technology (SUSTech) and the University of Hong Kong (HKU). Our team took part in both stages — at SUSTech and at HKU — learning from multiple iGEM teams across the Asia-Pacific region and polishing our project together through lectures, mock reviews, project presentations and interactive activities.

 SUSTech stage: mock review and project exchange

 At SUSTech, we first attended several expert lectures covering frontier advances in synthetic biology, project validation methods and standardized practice. Then iGEM competition staff introduced the competition process and judging criteria online, giving us a clearer understanding of this year's rules and scoring priorities. The mock review was the highlight of the exchange. We went through the complete formal defense process, reviewing item by item from problem definition and technical route to validation design. The reviewers advised us to focus further on application scenarios, supplement data evidence and pay attention to compliant expression. We recorded these suggestions one by one and marked them as a list for the next round of improvement. In the project exchange session, we introduced the origins, core mechanism and expected application of the influenza A air-detection project, and also learned about other teams' technical routes and validation ideas. We also studied the design and content organization of each team's poster — from layout and figures to the story line — and gained a good deal of presentation experience.

 Interactive activities: making the project “playable, tangible and open to feedback”

 We applied to the conference for on-site interactive activities and were given a dedicated space to set up two sessions. The first was question cards designed around the target prizes: other teams could look at our questions according to their own target prize and write down their answers, while we explained on site the design idea behind each card and the judging criteria of concern, collecting cross-team feedback at the same time. The second turned the project design concept into a “Plane War”-style mini-game, in which participants advanced through levels following the story guide and learned about our project in a relaxed atmosphere. Player feedback will also be used to optimize the story pacing and level design. In addition, during exchanges on experimental experience, we swapped many practical tips with other teams on experimental design, quality control and data handling.

 HKU stage: lectures and a discussion session with past participants

 At the University of Hong Kong, we continued to attend expert lectures and joined a discussion session with past iGEM participants. Everyone exchanged views on what they gained from the competition, teamwork, and the difficulties encountered in advancing projects and how they were solved. The sharing by past participants gave us a more concrete understanding of iGEM's rhythm and priorities. The HKU stage also included a poster display and free-exchange session, where we had in-depth discussions with other teams on project design, validation plans and presentation approaches.

 Outcomes and follow-up

 APiC was our first time taking part in an Asia-Pacific-level iGEM team conference, and it was also the very first edition. Through the two stages at SUSTech and HKU, we not only learned from frontier lectures and review experience, but also helped more teams learn about our project through the interactive activities we designed ourselves. Next, we will optimize the validation plan and interaction design according to the reviewers' suggestions and what we gained from the exchange, refine the project materials, and continue to stay in contact and cooperate with the other teams.

 What we shared

 We likewise prioritized synthetic biology in our outreach, explaining its application prospects in drug synthesis, biological detection and other directions in the context of the current state of the pharmaceutical industry, so that the students could see the frontier directions their major can extend into. Through accessible examples, we showed that synthetic biology combines playful creativity with scientific rigor, dispelling the students' fear of frontier research and letting them know that young students can likewise carry out hands-on synthetic biology innovation projects.

 iGEM and youth science innovation

 We introduced the iGEM competition and the value of science-innovation practice for young people, explaining that the competition is not just laboratory experiments but a science-innovation activity combining experiments, hardware, design and social research.  We also shared the team's human practices, academic exchanges and competition experience, and distributed custom merchandise to boost on-site engagement.  Outreach for Freshmen: Explaining Synthetic Biology to Future Explorers

 Classroom exchange 

 The session was delivered jointly by the iGEM student team and our supervising teachers. We started from synthetic biology itself to help the students build a basic understanding: it is not just gene editing, but requires the engineering mindset of “design–build–test–learn” to solve real-world problems in healthcare, environmental governance, new materials development and so on.  What we introduced

 We started from synthetic biology itself to help the students build a basic understanding: it is not just gene editing, but requires the engineering mindset of “design–build–test–learn” to solve real problems in healthcare, environmental governance and new materials development. We used everyday examples to break the stereotype that “synthetic biology is far removed from daily life”, letting the students feel that the discipline is both fun and rigorous.

 We introduced research projects, academic competitions, academic conferences and social practice, emphasizing that extracurricular practice needs to be built on a solid foundation in professional courses. Since most of the students had never encountered iGEM, we introduced this global synthetic biology student competition: it is not limited to wet-lab work but also covers hardware construction, modeling, human practices, wiki development and poster defense, and can develop a comprehensive skill set covering literature research, experimental operation, stakeholder interviews and teamwork.

 Project presentation and outcomes

 We focused on introducing the airborne influenza A virus biosensor project, explaining its positioning for infection-risk early warning in enclosed spaces, and shared our interview experience with experts in disease control, the health commission, hardware development and synthetic biology, showing that expert opinions were genuinely applied to project iteration. During the activity we distributed an influenza A prevention handbook written by the team, linking laboratory research with public health needs; the supervising teachers also added explanations of the relationship between professional coursework and science-innovation practice.

 In terms of outcomes, most participants built a basic understanding of synthetic biology, the iGEM competition and the influenza A detection sensor project, gained preliminary knowledge of genetic engineering, and dispelled the fixed impression that “research is very distant from young learners”. The activity also completed influenza A prevention outreach and raised the students' awareness of respiratory infectious disease prevention. The shortcoming was that, limited by the length of the talk, we could not explain the experimental principles and hardware details in depth, and the handbook was distributed in bulk without item-by-item explanation on site. Going forward we plan to open a consultation channel for interested participants, hold small themed salons, and collect feedback on the handbook to iterate subsequent outreach materials.
