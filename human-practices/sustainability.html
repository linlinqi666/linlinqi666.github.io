Sustainability

A smoke alarm, not a stethoscope

If Echo Yeast had to be summed up in one metaphor, it is closer to a smoke alarm than to a stethoscope.

Most influenza A testing today works like a stethoscope: it only hears the virus after it has already flared up inside a person and crossed the detection threshold. What we set out to build is a smoke alarm, one that smells the first wisp of smoke in the air before a fire starts. What it offers is an early warning at the level of space, not a diagnostic conclusion for an individual — we have been clear about that from day one.

One device, four goals

The idea began as an engineering choice. But when the team seriously asked what it means for sustainable development, it turned out to touch four goals at once: SDG 3 Good Health and Well-being, SDG 4 Quality Education, SDG 9 Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure, and SDG 17 Partnerships for the Goals. We did not arrive at those four by mood: the team worked through all 17 UN Sustainable Development Goals with their sub-targets, item by item, to see which ones our project could stand behind with evidence and which it could not. The map below holds the honest result — four tiles lit, thirteen left dark on purpose — and everything beneath it is what those four tiles are built on.

This page lays out, goal by goal, what we actually did over the past year: which external suggestions rewrote the design criteria one by one, how far validation has advanced since, and which gates we are still working through. Interviews, inter-team exchanges, school outreach and literature checks came one after another, pushing the plan from its first conception to what it is today — and the plan is still moving.

Seventeen goals, four commitments

4 of 17 lit — the rest are left unlit on purpose. A tile is only coloured when a section below can show the work behind it.

The four we lit are not four separate stories. Read in order, they are one chain: SDG 3 is the problem worth solving, SDG 4 is the audience that must understand it, SDG 9 is whether we can build it and afford to, and SDG 17 is the partners who pushed the plan from first idea to what it is now. The dark tiles are not rejected — most of them we simply have no evidence to stand behind yet, and an unlit tile is cheaper than a claim we cannot keep.

SDG 3Good Health and Well-being

Targets: 3.d · 3.3 — one of the four lit tiles in the map above.

We put SDG 3 first because the problem the project sets out to solve is singular: influenza A outbreaks are discovered too late.

Why influenza A deserves an earlier step

Before writing this section, we went through the 17 UN goals together with their sub-targets, item by item, to see which one genuinely holds our work. The closest match is 3.d: strengthening the capacity of all countries, in particular developing countries, for early warning, risk reduction and management of national and global health risks. It asks for three things — early warning, risk reduction and management capacity — and our device answers the first, which happens to be the hardest of the three to turn into a concrete tool, that is, moving the warning point from "already outbreak" to "before it enters the body". 3.3, ending the epidemics of communicable diseases, is where this line ultimately leads, not something we can claim as done today. We therefore place ourselves at 3.d rather than 3.3: the difference is that 3.d is what we are doing, and 3.3 is where we want to arrive.

Influenza A is good at hiding. Its natural reservoir sits in wild water birds, and most avian-origin influenza viruses neither infect people nor sustain human-to-human transmission. But the influenza genome is segmented: once viruses of different origins meet inside the same host cell, genetic reassortment can produce new viruses against which the population has no pre-existing immunity, and among the seasonal viruses that already transmit steadily between people, hemagglutinin keeps undergoing antigenic drift — reagents designed against one subtype are always chasing the virus from behind. How the design criteria came to stop chasing subtypes will be told further on.

Its rhythm makes things worse: cases surge in the epidemic season, public vigilance fades once the season passes, and when the next season comes the mutated virus returns, while existing detection mostly fires only after the virus has broken out inside the body and crossed a threshold — like starting to watch from the second half of the season every year. The value of one step earlier is to move the moment from "already outbreak" to "before it enters the body". The scale behind that rhythm is not in dispute: WHO's 2025 fact sheet puts seasonal influenza at an estimated one billion cases a year, and surveillance in the WHO South-East Asia Region attributes about 66% of positive samples to influenza A (FluNet weekly records, 2025). We deliberately do not multiply the two into a global influenza-A count — 66% is a regional share, and the error of applying it worldwide cannot be estimated — so this page keeps only the two numbers that each carry their own source.

Detection is where that rhythm bites. The virus returns once a year, and each season the same question has to be answered again: how to find it sooner. Three technical routes are already established, each holding a different position in the chain from sample to result, and each carries its own weaknesses. Their limits are worth stating plainly, because the one position none of them holds is the position this project moves into.

qPCR is the most sensitive and quantitative, the accepted reference standard — Merckx and colleagues' 2017 systematic review used RT-PCR as the yardstick against which the rapid tests were measured. The price is that it cannot do without laboratory equipment and trained staff, and a routine run takes about one to two hours. ELISA suits batch screening: high throughput and low cost, but its sensitivity falls behind nucleic-acid amplification, and it needs a reader. Colloidal gold strips are the fastest: a result on site in 10–15 minutes with no instruments, at the most direct price of limited sensitivity, uncertain readings, and results that usually need confirmatory retesting.

We checked two systematic reviews on the accuracy of the strips. The 2012 meta-analysis in the Annals of Internal Medicine by Chartrand and colleagues pooled 159 studies and 26 rapid influenza diagnostic tests: combined sensitivity 62.3% (95% CI 57.9%–66.6%), combined specificity 98.2% (97.5%–98.7%), and the authors' own conclusion that influenza "can be ruled in but not ruled out through the use of RIDTs". Sensitivity for influenza A in that review was 64.6% (CI 59.0%–70.1%), and lower still in adults at 53.9% (47.9%–59.8%). Merckx and colleagues widened the scope to 162 studies in 2017, and the pooled sensitivity of traditional rapid tests for influenza A fell to 54.4% (CrI 48.9%–59.8%); novel digital immunoassays and rapid nucleic-acid amplification tests, by that paper's statement, were "markedly higher sensitive... with equally high specificities". Both reviews report specificities above 98%; sensitivity is their shared weakness. Back to the three routes: qPCR, ELISA and colloidal gold strips solve laboratory precision, batch throughput and on-site speed respectively, and none of them covers the one position that matters here — in the air, before the virus enters a body. That is the gap we formally brought into design consideration. The hardware side aims at the same gap: a wet-wall cyclone pulls airborne particles straight into liquid and enriches them slowly, because capture efficiency, rather than detection sensitivity, sets the ceiling of the whole chain — a conclusion also written down in our hardware development log.

What a smoke alarm looks like

The device can be explained starting from the air. Air is swirled at high speed by a wet-wall cyclone, throwing its particles — including any influenza virus present — straight into the culture liquid in the collection cup; the engineered yeast in the cup recognises the virus with nanobodies displayed on its surface; the recognition signal is amplified inside the cell and converted into fluorescence we can read; and the device completes the readout on site. Four steps in series, from one particle of air to one early warning. What the cup holds is a sample of the air a person breathes, not a sample of the person: a clinical conclusion about one individual is drawn from that individual's own sample and read against clinical standards, and a reading of a room cannot stand in for it. The warning therefore stays where the sample was taken — at the level of the space.

The premise is that the air does contain virus that can be intercepted. To establish whether this air pathway holds, we returned to the review Tellier published in 2006 in the American journal Emerging Infectious Diseases, which sorted through the published evidence supporting aerosol transmission; its statement that "Published evidence indicates that aerosol transmission of influenza can be an important mode of transmission" means that aerosol transmission of influenza can be an important mode of spread, not a marginal case. The same review also reminds readers that aerosols, large droplets and direct contact with secretions "are not mutually exclusive": air is one route among several, and the device watches that one route from the outside. What happens once the virus is already inside a person remains the business of the established routes, each of which reads a sample taken from the body; the device reads the space before that point. The two therefore cover different stretches of the same chain, and both are kept.

Why the recognition unit aims at the conserved stem region of HA instead of chasing subtypes: those two criteria do not come from the literature, they are first-hand conclusions from interviews, and two experts each contributed half. Wenjie Wang, director of the medical technology department at a Shenzhen hospital, supplied the clinical half — the situation on the front line, in his words, is that "clinical typing demand is limited": hospitals focus on testing whether the virus is positive or negative in order to guide treatment, while typing is published by the CDC, so he suggested we focus on "detecting virus in the air" without distinguishing subtypes. Shisong Fang, deputy director of the pathogen institute at the Shenzhen CDC, supplied the molecular-design half: for type diagnosis, "conserved genes such as M/NP/NS should be preferred", and for antigen expression, "conserved regions at the neck of HA should be preferred"; he even left the method behind — reference sequences from the last three to five years, use BLAST to screen for conserved fragments of around 200 bp, and use mosaic algorithms to cover circulating strains when necessary. Together the two turned "lock the conserved region, play down subtypes" from a slogan into a design choice with a basis.

Choosing yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae, chassis BY4741) as the sensing element is less a quirk than a division of labour. Conventional test reagents are single-use: every test consumes a fresh batch of antibodies and colour developers. Living cells renew themselves; as long as they are alive, they keep translating molecular signals into fluorescence we can read. In this system the nanobody is the recognition element: it binds the hemagglutinin (HA) of influenza A virus with high specificity, and the binding event itself is the switch — the bound HA displaces the MT1 auto-inhibitory peptide away from hM1Dq through steric hindrance, releasing the receptor inhibition so the downstream cascade runs and the readout is lifted.

What the front line rewrote

Design criteria were set, but criteria are not feasibility. We put the approach in turn to clinicians, the CDC and the health commission, asking the same question: does it hold up in a real setting? Their corrections landed on four levels — technical metrics, detection target, usage scenario and compliance boundary.

The in-vitro diagnostics industry as it stands only acts after a virus has broken out inside the body and crossed a threshold, so moving the line of defence earlier does carry public-health value — that was the reassuring part of Wenjie Wang's answer when we put the approach to clinicians. He then pointed out three hard constraints: many variables in the air, low concentrations in open environments, and a possibly limited effective range for a sensor — and he singled out the false-positive rates of self-test reagents on the market, which can reach 50%–70%. We took that in: specificity and threshold decision moved from bonus features to primary optimisation targets, and the positioning of "environmental screening, not diagnosis" has since been written into every product statement. Mistaking a screening result for a diagnosis costs far more than a missed detection. After the meeting we copied the three points he raised into a design constraint table.

This one lands most concretely on sustainability: once a false positive is treated as a diagnosis, what follows is unnecessary class suspension and repeated disinfection, burning public resources and public trust. Nailing the positioning as environmental warning and putting specificity first closes that chain of waste at its source.

The three constraints he raised all sit downstream of one prior question: is there virus in the air to capture at all? Kai Fu took that question as his own. To find out whether live, capturable virus is really present in the air, we looked up the 2018 PNAS work by Yan and colleagues: they screened 355 volunteers with acute respiratory symptoms, 142 of whom had confirmed influenza, and collected paired nasopharyngeal swabs and 30-minute exhaled-breath samples separated by particle size; 52 (39%) of the fine-particle aerosols grew live virus in culture, as did 150 (89%) of the swabs, and the geometric mean of RNA copies in fine particles per 30 minutes was 3.8×10⁴. The paper's key finding is that neither coughing nor sneezing is necessary for producing infectious aerosols — in the authors' words, "sneezing and coughing were not necessary for infectious aerosol generation". The 2020 Nature Medicine work by Leung and colleagues extends the same picture across ages: they detected seasonal coronaviruses, influenza viruses and rhinoviruses in the exhaled breath and cough samples of children and adults, so the material a breathing person releases is detectable in both groups. Taken together, the two works show that exhaled breath does contain capturable virus, and that this does not depend on the patient coughing at the time.

On that basis the priority direction was set: a single-person exhaled-breath mode, supplemented by an enrichment device, with environmental sampling as a backstop — Kai Fu's suggestion, and the one we adopted. He also left two questions we have not finished answering. The first is timeliness: the yeast signalling pathway needs roughly one to two hours from receiving a signal to expressing fluorescence, which weakens the point of an early warning, and we are modifying the signalling pathway to compress it. The second is cost: the hardware has to be affordable at the primary-care level, and the magnitude he gave was about 10 RMB for an active-breathing device, which we are working towards. He split his suggestions into "can do now" and "cannot do yet", and we have arranged the order of validation accordingly.

Where the device should land was answered from the people side: it is essentially a detection reagent rather than a drug, it belongs to the prevention stage, and children are a high-incidence group for influenza A, so institutions should come first — schools are the most welcoming, medical institutions the most popular, and government can reduce medical-insurance spending, with households and individuals last; that ordering came from Manqing Deng. The regulatory boundary was drawn just as plainly: there is no official standard in China for airborne virus detection, so such a device cannot serve as a basis for medical diagnosis and can only perform environmental screening and risk warning — Yiwu Luo of the approval division of the Shenzhen Health Commission made the limit explicit. On rigour, the clinical mainstream is dual influenza A plus B testing, and target genes should favour conserved regions such as M, NP and NS; while the bottleneck of the whole air-sampling chain, in Shisong Fang's words, is that "if the front end cannot capture, the back-end detection is useless however advanced".

The three opinions synthesise into a single positioning: environmental risk warning only, no diagnosis, with the first batch landing in institutions — schools, medical institutions and government. It is also an allocation of resources: with the bottleneck at front-end capture, funding and manpower should not keep piling onto back-end sensitivity, so we moved enrichment and capture to the top of the validation checklist and wrote dual detection into the next phase of planning. Institutions first also means the first batch of installations stays a controllable number — pressing the false-positive rate down in a small range before talking about scaling up saves more resources than covering every scenario at once, and is less likely to spend the public's trust all in one go.

The sustainability meaning of the front-line round comes in layers. Single-person exhalation replaces high-flow environmental sampling, and the air path and consumables shrink with it. The order of 10 RMB decides whether the device can enter a primary-care clinic at all. The one-to-two-hour timeliness decides whether the warning still means anything — if the readout comes out slower than the transmission, the consumables saved do not buy the time back. The two questions Kai Fu left open — cost and timeliness — therefore stay on our own checklist as the next measurable stops, to be answered before any talk of wider deployment.

SDG 4Quality Education

Target: 4.7 — one of the four lit tiles in the map above.

Explaining the project clearly is harder than getting it built.

A classroom before the laboratory

When the 2026 cohort of freshmen enrolled, we brought the whole thing into the classroom. Team members stood at the front; sitting in front of them were students who had just entered the university and had not yet entered a laboratory. We started from synthetic biology itself: it approaches real problems in medicine and the environment with the engineering cycle of "design, build, test, learn", and a global event like iGEM lays out hardware, modelling, human practices, the wiki and the poster defence together, letting the freshmen see what innovation outside the textbook looks like. When it came to the project itself, we only stated the positioning: a device that gives early warning of influenza risk in enclosed spaces, and how we truly fed the advice of CDC, health-commission, hardware and synthetic-biology experts into our iterations. The screen first stopped at the "Signal Sensing Module", laying out the PAGER receptor's α-factor, MT1, hM1Dq and the HA-recognising nanobody; the next screen was the "Signal Transduction Module", with the before-and-after comparison of GPA1's KIGII-to-EYNLV change drawn on it, so the freshmen left with an overall impression of the two parts. We also handed out the team's self-written influenza protection handbook, tying laboratory research to public-health needs. Limited by time, the experimental principles and hardware details could not be expanded, but this is exactly the first impression we wanted to leave the freshmen: synthetic biology is not distant, and it is both interesting and rigorous.

Questions that missed the technology

The questions afterwards often did not land on the technology. More people wanted to know how it differs from existing tests, whether yeast is safe, and how much it would really cost to install one in a classroom. Those questions forced us to compress recognition, switch and reporter into one plain sentence, and made us re-confirm for ourselves which part of the design is most easily misunderstood.

This line lands on target 4.7 of SDG 4. Checking the UN entry for 4.7, what it asks is to ensure that all learners acquire the knowledge and skills needed to promote sustainable development; on that basis we turned science communication into something concrete — carrying the common knowledge of synthetic biology and public health into the classroom, so that people meeting the field for the first time can also take away one sentence they can understand. The same plain sentence also keeps the device honest after it leaves the laboratory: a reader who understands that the warning describes a space rather than a person is one fewer false alarm for the project to answer for.

SDG 9Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure

Targets: 9.4 · 9.5 — one of the four lit tiles in the map above.

Getting the recognition unit built is only the first step. For the device to be deployable for real it has to pass three gates: can it be built, is it affordable to use, and can the approach be verified. And this is a route we opened ourselves, with no precedent to copy; of the three gates, building is only the first, and the longer road is to falsify or confirm, one by one, the premise that living cells can really do the sensing for us — which is exactly where 9.4's preference for verifying first, rather than rushing to build the full device, comes from.

A lower threshold, and still verifiable

Looking back, the point of this device that convinces us most is that a "living" system lowers the threshold of testing while remaining verifiable. From start to finish it is positioned as environmental screening and risk warning, not diagnosis — that was settled in SDG 3 and is not repeated here; only its industrial meaning is added.

Yeast was not picked casually. The reasons rank as follows: it is cheap and easy to culture; and it is itself a eukaryote, so the mature signal-transduction network inside the cell can be borrowed directly. Two more are often overlooked: it is a generally-recognised-as-safe organism and tolerates the environment well, so deployment needs little worry; and its toolbox is complete, so installing a recognition unit is a routine operation. Taken together, these turn living-cell sensing from an idea into an executable plan. The hardware side carries this on — changing the target requires only changing the strain, while the temperature-control and optical modules are reused as they are; "the reagents grow themselves" refers to this living system.

In hardware the device falls into three blocks: a heating block that holds the yeast steady at 30 °C (specification ±0.5 °C), a pluggable optical path with 485 nm excitation and 528 nm reception, its two optical axes at 90° to suppress excitation crosstalk, and a signal-acquisition circuit that turns the photocurrent of a photodiode into readable numbers. These three blocks have completed module definition and interface alignment to the integrated interface specification. The numbers for the integrated device — enrichment efficiency and detection limit — are not yet available; temperature control has so far been verified at the level of parts rather than of the assembled device, as the hardware log below shows. Verification of the integrated device follows the rhythm of "core first, device later": the upstream living-cell sensing chain (the chassis three-gene knockout, GPA1 humanisation and PAGER functional validation) is still being completed, and until the upstream works, readings of enrichment and detection cannot stand; display and human-machine interaction are scheduled after that, according to the hardware specifications. This is the bill we owe for the next step — once the core chain is through, enrichment efficiency and detection limit go straight into measurement, temperature-control precision is calibrated with them, and we will come back then to fill in the hard numbers for this section.

One gate had to be passed before any claim of novelty: academia may already have similar work using yeast surface display to detect H1N1, and without searching and comparing, we cannot explain what is new in our own approach. Honghua Jia, our synthetic-biology consultant, raised the gate, and we treated it as a required step rather than a suggestion. We really did search both the surface-display and membrane-bound routes, and did find the 2024 work by Seo and colleagues published in ACS Synthetic Biology: they used Saccharomyces cerevisiae surface display of a VHH-type Quenchbody, displaying and screening 17 antibody fragments targeting the H1N1 hemagglutinin (HA), with a quantitative detection range for H1N1-HA of 0.5–16 μg/mL (half-maximal concentration 2.60 μg/mL) and a detection range for virus particles of 2.4×10⁴ to 1.5×10⁷ PFU/mL, showing high specificity for the HA of other influenza A subtypes; by that paper's statement that it circumvents "the need for transport of analytes into biosensor cells", what it bypasses is the requirement that the analyte must enter the interior of the cell. This gives us a ready-made reference: "letting the virus be recognised without entering the cell" has already been made into one version by others; the difference between the membrane-bound and surface-display paradigms is something we must compare head-on in the paper, not step around. That reminder was given in an online meeting.

What this reminder saved is the investment most easily wasted: acting before searching could easily put funding and person-months into a route others have already completed. Only after searching did we know that "letting the virus stay out of the cell" already has a version, and that our task is to make the difference clear on the membrane-bound route. For sustainability this too is a kind of efficiency — not saving materials, but saving duplicated work.

Targets 9.5 and 9.4 were chosen by comparing the UN sub-targets item by item. 9.5 asks for enhanced scientific research, upgraded technological capabilities of industrial sectors and encouragement of innovation; it lands on our recognition and signal-transduction design itself — installing a recognition unit into yeast and having it work on the cell surface is foundational technological capacity-building. 9.4 asks for infrastructure and industry to be upgraded with greater resource efficiency and cleaner technology; it constrains our trade-off — preferring to verify the premise first rather than rushing to build the full device, because a device built with unverified efficiency is only a consumption of resources.

Iterating in plastic, verifying in pieces

The hardware log keeps the dates. The collection cup was drawn in AutoCAD on 14 September, long and propped up by a supporting skirt; the SolidWorks model of 21 September dropped the skirt, shortened the cone and enlarged the base radius for a rounder body; the version of 22 September cut an optical window into the conical surface, because the cyclone leaves only a shallow depth of liquid and the readout has to happen where the liquid actually is. Three design decisions, three prints, ten days.

Heat was verified as a chain of parts before any device existed. A PT100 was taped to the heating film with heat-resistant tape and a dab of thermal silicone, and the controller was set to hold 30 °C with the duty cycle capped at 30% so as not to kill an IRF520 that had no heatsink yet. The first run started at 26.78 °C and overshot to 31.36 °C — about 1.4 °C — before the controller walked the duty cycle back down through 26.3%, 21.5%, 20.2%, 18.9% and 16.1%. The direction of the closed loop was right; the fine tuning belongs to the aluminium block that had not yet arrived.

The board was built out of named modules — a LMS2596 step-down, an IRF520, a MAX31865, the Nano, an AMS1117 regulator with an LDOA6 constant-current driver and an ADS1115. When the self-test found the I2C bus silent, the log records the path: A4 and A5 read as analog inputs returned unstable voltages (2.40 V, 5.00 V, 3.11 V — the signature of a floating line); continuity checks passed; SDA-to-VDD and SCL-to-VDD then measured a few milliohms instead of 10 kΩ, which is a solder bridge, not a dead chip. Clearing it produced the reply "found device 0x48". When the Nano itself later burned out, the log lists four candidate causes and a replacement procedure — verify the buck output, check every rail and signal line for shorts, then power up one module at a time. The LDOA6 was retired outright: its current could not be tuned (fixed at 14.7 mA), and a brightness that can be neither raised nor lowered cannot serve a signal-to-noise trade-off.

What the log buys in sustainability terms is specific. Each design assumption was tested as a physical object within days, so a wrong assumption costs a few grams of filament rather than a re-tooled assembly. The cheapest premise — can we hold 30 °C at all — was verified before the rest of the device was allowed to exist. A fault that is measured before it is repaired does not consume a second board, and a module that cannot be adjusted is retired instead of tolerated. The log itself is part of the handover: the next person can re-run the same diagnosis in minutes instead of starting from a smoking board.

Verify the premise first: the cell wall

Installing the recognition unit into yeast and letting living cells do the sensing is something we wrote into SDG 9 because it forces us to think clearly about one question: does the living-cell route really save resources. A living system that renews itself and keeps translating molecular signals into fluorescence readings replaces the traditional reagents that consume a fresh batch of antibodies and colour developers with every test — and the premise of "saving" is that it actually works. But living cells also bring a premise traditional reagents do not have: the virus must first cross the comparatively thick cell wall of the yeast before it can reach the receptor on the membrane. At first we only stared at "does it recognise", never asking "can it reach"; it was Xinyuan Qiu, our synthetic-biology expert, who pointed out this long-overlooked gap: whether an intact virus can cross the cell wall to reach the membrane directly decides whether the device can work, and "strong antigen–antibody specificity" is not the same thing as "high sensitivity" — sensitivity has to be defined as "the smallest number of detectable particles per unit volume", and these quantitative bases are exactly what we need to build from scratch.

The Human Practices page records whom we consulted and what we learned; what this section has to say is another thing — why what we learned decides whether our sustainability route holds. The path he gave was to get it running first and optimise later: run the system through with antigen-epitope short peptides first, and if cell-wall penetration does not hold, decisively consider changing the chassis rather than continuing to invest in complex engineering. We followed that order, and it agrees with the strategy on the hardware side — temperature control and the optical path first use standard cuvettes to keep uncertainty out of the optical debugging, and only then swap in the self-made collection cup. Only when this step is taken steadily does 9.5's technological capacity-building become discussable: if the living-cell route truly holds, we have installed "recognition plus amplification" into a reusable platform; if it does not hold and we are forced back to single-use reagents, the resource consumption that 9.4 wants to push down comes straight back. This gate is therefore not an experimental detail — it directly decides whether this route holds up in sustainability.

On the receptor route, the same preliminary step was raised from two directions — first modify yeast's endogenous Ste2, and leave the human GPCR for later — by Zhouqing Luo of Xiamen University and Hui Wu of Dalian University of Technology. This advice was not made on feel — we searched for whether anyone had put GPCR signal transduction and yeast surface display together for detection, and found the 2021 work by Maneira and colleagues in FEMS Yeast Research, whose case study was SARS-CoV-2. Its statement, "active virus detection through a modulated signal activated by a GPCR agonist, whose concentration correlates to the viral titer", says exactly this: let the agonist concentration correspond to the viral titre, and use concentration to activate a modulated signal; the same paper also leaves the judgement, "still limited to indirect antibody recognition", that yeast-based viral diagnosis platforms at the time were still limited to indirect antibody recognition. The two layers must be read separately: the GPCR route does work in yeast, and someone has verified that; but linking recognition and signal transduction into one complete chain is neither their achievement nor ours yet — that step we have to walk ourselves. On the order of validation, we first complete one full pathway and achieve a visible response, and only then talk about swapping components. That advice was settled in an online discussion with Hui Wu; in the same meeting we walked through the whole design from the principles of PAGER onwards.

Modifying the endogenous receptor first and touching the human GPCR later is itself a form of stopping losses: the endogenous pathway is ready-made, cheap to modify and quick to verify, while the human GPCR still has the extra problem of coupling to solve, and starting before the premise holds risks the investment coming to nothing. With the order right, the same budget walks several more steps.

Methodology needed its own homework. The first suggestion was to make good use of limited data: use the first- and second-order derivatives of OD and fluorescence time series for prediction, and statistical hypothesis tests to give p-values, so that early data can project later trends, serving the shortening of detection time directly — that came from Haili Zhang, our modelling advisor. On the molecular-docking side, the models were to be rebuilt with AlphaFold 3.0, MM/GBSA free-energy decomposition and molecular-dynamics simulations, closing with SPR or MST wet experiments to connect calculation and experiment; Xiaolong Liu set that bar. Both were done online: the data curves were reviewed on the shared screen, and the pages of docking results were confirmed item by item in the same remote session.

What the two methodology suggestions save is repeated experiments: if early data can be extrapolated, every time point does not have to be redone; if calculation screens out the impossible candidates before the wet experiments, that saves far more than laying out the full suite of experiments from the start.

A weak signal is another problem to solve, and the way proposed was to step around it: borrow yeast's native α-factor cell-communication pathway and build inter-cell communication and positive-feedback amplification into the system, so that even a small amount of activation can be seen — Zhouqing Luo's suggestion. On the hardware-engineering side, Rongkang Deng, a manager at a biotechnology company in Shenzhen, ran the time accounts of the whole chain and judged that a complete prototype in two or three months was unrealistic; he suggested first producing a working-principle block diagram and modular schematics, making one local core-function demonstration piece, and preferring to reuse mature modules rather than develop everything in-house. His judgement on the enrichment bottleneck agrees with Shisong Fang's: the virus particles are too small, and direct aeration into the reaction liquid mostly escapes as bubbles, while prolonged aeration evaporates the liquid — a stretch that has to be solved by engineering means.

The judgement that a complete prototype cannot be made in two or three months, together with the preference for reusing mature modules, changed our route from all in-house to borrowing what can be borrowed and developing in-house only what must be. The most direct sustainability meaning of that change: the more modules developed in-house, the more each iteration costs and the fewer trial-and-error rounds there are; after reusing mature parts, the same budget can run several more rounds of validation. Block diagram first, then a local demonstration piece, follows the same logic — taking the uncertain parts apart and verifying them piece by piece before the full device takes shape, so as to avoid finishing the device only to find the premise does not hold.

Our expectations for the device do not stop at H1N1 either. The real value of the PAGER-Yeast platform lies in modularity and replaceability: the nanobody recognition unit on the membrane can iterate quickly against new antigens — swap the recognition head, and the same chassis points at the next target. Manqing Deng made the same point, suggesting that we make the biological components into "replaceable modules" so the approach can extend to other viruses, bacteria and even the detection of mycoplasma resistance.

SDG 17Partnerships for the Goals

Targets: 17.6 · 17.16 — one of the four lit tiles in the map above.

Almost every key decision in this project was pushed forward by external partners. People from different fields came in round after round, tearing the plan down and rebuilding it.

Every loop, a new partner

Clinicians, CDC and health-commission experts, hardware engineers, academic peers and the public came in round after round along the spiral of action research. Every round started from the same move: diagnose the problem clearly first, then set the plan, do the work, and afterwards look back at where we had judged wrongly — then enter the next round with new questions.

What team exchanges gave us

At the South China regional iGEM exchange, we presented the overall design from the yeast chassis through signal recognition to the reporter system on the big screen. The opening screen read "Welcome to the world of yeast", with the full name of the project as its subtitle: the construction and application of an influenza A virus biosensor based on Saccharomyces cerevisiae.

When the talk reached the reporter system, we put the FUS1-yEGFP and FUS1-lacZ readout routes side by side on the same screen, and that became the screen we were asked about most afterwards. The questions concentrated on low airborne virus concentration, yeast sensitivity, reporter leakage, and how we differ from conventional testing after all. The mock review that followed exposed things even more thoroughly: our answers were not good enough. Afterwards we sorted the questions out, and they fell roughly into four categories — experimental (yeast response time, signalling-pathway validation, virus concentration thresholds), mechanical and cost (model calculation, cost control, yeast replacement), application-scenario (where the device would be installed, data coverage for large scenarios, home self-testing), and the remaining questions on advantages over existing technology, actual use, similar research and biosafety approval. That list basically covers the key links from experiment to deployment.

As the meeting broke up, the names and doodles of the teams were left on the same background board, SZPU-CHINA among them.

Team-to-team exchange did not stop at that one meeting. The yeast-themed online workshop, initiated by CAU-China, went straight to the bench. Six teams took part — CAU-China, NEFU-China, SZPU-CHINA, Keystone, NKU and SHSID — each reporting its project design and experimental progress in turn before the floor opened. The discussion stayed on the difficulties that actually stall yeast work: transformation efficiency, gene knockout, reporter-system leakage, membrane-protein expression and localisation, product format and biosafety.

At the first Asia-Pacific iGEM Community Conference (APiC), held in two phases at SUSTech and HKU, the mock reviewers suggested that we focus the application scenario further, add data evidence and watch our compliance wording; we recorded every point as an item on the improvement list. We also applied for on-site interactive space and ran two sessions of our own design: the iGEM Project Prism Program, a card exercise in which each visiting team picked two award cards out of fifteen and worked through the ten questions on the back against its own evidence, and Yeast Defender, a mini-game that carries the project design through a playable story. Five award tracks came back filled in, the answers were collected into one bilingual record, and the card set and the process note stay available to any team that wants to run the same exercise.

The exchange that produced something lasting was a handbook. JLU-FBH (Jilin University) launched Together with iGEM, inviting each participating team to correct one common misconception about synthetic biology in plain language; the handbook brings together contributions from 17 iGEM teams worldwide, and SZPU-China joined as a contributing team. Our six entries all concern influenza A — that it is not "just a cold", that it does not spread only through coughing and sneezing, that antigen tests and nucleic-acid tests answer different questions, that children's cases warrant testing rather than observation alone, that environmental surveillance complements rather than replaces clinical testing, and that engineered yeast sealed inside an enclosed device is not an inherent hazard. Writing them forced us to restate, in language a reader outside the field can check, the same things we had said to experts.

Not appealing to authority

Xinyuan Qiu's remark that "expert statements and social practice cannot substitute for factual evidence; avoid appealing to authority" applies equally to ourselves: interview quotes can only serve as design criteria, never as project conclusions. This is also why this page repeatedly distinguishes literature from interviews: literature answers what the world is like, interviews answer what we should do in a specific situation, and neither may overstep its role.

He also gave us a more concrete sense of what kind of collaboration lasts. Contact with a contract manufacturer made us realise that the expectations of all parties need to be stated and aligned at the very start of a collaboration, for one-off cooperation to grow into long-term partnership.

A method to hand over

This action-research spiral combined with inter-team mutual review is the part we consider most reusable: record the questions of every round of interviews, the criteria formed from them, and the plan finally changed, and those who come after can follow the records through our process of judgement instead of retrying from zero.

This method lands on target 17.16: enhancing the global partnership for sustainable development, complemented by multi-stakeholder partnerships that mobilise and share knowledge, expertise, technology and financial resources. We confirmed this target after comparing the sub-targets item by item: our collaborators span clinical practice, the CDC, the health commission, industry and academia, and what is shared is not funding but standards of judgement and technical experience. Target 17.6 asks for cooperation in science, technology and innovation and the promotion of knowledge sharing — which is almost exactly our daily routine over this past year. The routine also ran outward, not only inward: a card exercise and a mini-game we designed for other teams at APiC, six plain-language entries in a handbook co-edited with JLU-FBH and 16 other iGEM teams, and bench-level trouble-shooting swapped in the yeast workshop. In each case what left our hands was something another team could pick up and use — that is the shape partnership took for us.

Summary

Taken together, the four goals show that the road already walked, the direction now being pushed forward, and the costs that must be taken seriously are all plainer than they were in the project's first, optimistic version.

Where impact lands

Where the impact finally lands depends on how widely the device can be deployed. Really installed in fever clinics and schools, the moment at which a cluster outbreak is discovered has a chance to move substantially earlier; the warning here is always at the environmental level and non-diagnostic. A located warning also shapes the response that follows it: disinfection and control can be targeted at the space and the moment flagged, and the indiscriminate versions — whole-building spraying and blanket drug use — become unnecessary. This upside stops at a single premise: deployment density decides the scale of the benefit, and density can only be accumulated bit by bit through measurement and pilots.

Costs we watch closely

We fill the cost column in full too. The risk most in need of vigilance is misuse. Once environmental screening is treated as a diagnostic conclusion, or excessive false positives trigger unnecessary class suspensions, what is wasted is not only resources but the public's trust — which is why the positioning of environmental screening and risk warning must appear in every product statement. The remaining two belong to technical premise and baseline: the premise of "can the virus cross the cell wall" is verified before any further investment goes in, so the route never rests on an assumption; and biosafety is written in as a design requirement from the start.

The next measurable stop

The costs and risks are not abstract. They are already scattered through the four sections above: from the reliability of the detection line, the premise of cell-wall penetration, to whether expert judgement holds up in real scenarios, to the boundaries of the interview material itself. Two of them are already written as numbers we can be measured against: the roughly 10 RMB cost target for an active-breathing device, and the one-to-two-hour gap between signal and readout that any early warning has to fit inside. What we have to do is turn one of them into the next stop that can be measured and answered — and then the next one.
